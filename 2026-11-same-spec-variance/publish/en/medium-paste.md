# The Same Spec, Ten Runs, Part 3 — Variance: All Ten Passed, So Where Does the Next Piece of Evidence Come From?

> **TL;DR** — Four briefs, ten runs each, and all forty programs passed the checks. Should the next step be more runs, a different batch of tasks, stronger acceptance checks, or having a person actually take the work over? Every one of these costs money, and each answers a different question. This piece is written for the people who design evaluations and for engineering managers, and it works backward from one question: which decision is the next piece of evidence supposed to change? It first separates repeating the same task, rewriting the document, and changing the task or environment, then explains why ten passes out of ten still cannot show that the four briefs are equally good. It then uses the example of "all three deliveries succeed" versus "pick one success out of three" to show that a success rate has to be read together with the way the work is used. The second half returns to practice: how to read two programs that both passed, how to preserve the old results, and which work and human costs a new comparison has to record. The next round's P/Q design compares the shared requirements with and without an optional self-check; it has not been executed, and the 288 scheduled slots are only a budget draft. Required behaviors that have already been decided still have to be honored, but if the question is whether to make the extra self-check a team default, the data we have is not enough.

> Series: [Overview](https://medium.com/p/3700b5d771fd) → [Part 1, Specification](https://medium.com/p/3c356327ea3c) → [Part 2, Contract](https://medium.com/p/97d5d4dc4fa8) → **Part 3, Variance (this piece)**. Chinese edition: [同一份規格，跑十次（三）：變異篇—十次都通過，下一筆證據該怎麼找？](https://medium.com/p/785ace43b955).

## 1. All four groups passed ten out of ten, so where does the next budget go?

Suppose a team is patching a Python tool and adds six self-check questions to the brief, to be answered before the work is handed in. The next program passes, and someone proposes that from now on every job of this kind should carry those six questions. This is the hypothetical scenario from section 2 of [the Overview](https://medium.com/p/3700b5d771fd). The question worth pressing is about the next assignment: will these six questions keep helping, or did they merely happen to accompany this one success?

The Overview split the problem into three layers. The requirements are first confirmed by whoever has the authority to decide, the owner; each program awaiting acceptance, the candidate, is then checked against those requirements. This piece addresses the third layer: once the requirements are clear and can be checked, is a newly added self-check worth using again and again? Required behaviors and compatibility rules must be recorded and checked in any case; they do not wait for proof of an average benefit. What needs comparing is the optional self-check that is attached on top while the requirements stay the same.

This layer has two main readers. The person who designs the evaluation and maintains the acceptance assets, whom this piece calls the eval owner, has to explain what judgment each result can support. The EM (engineering manager) allocates budget and people, and decides whether to adopt the self-check, keep the current policy or withdraw it. Both have to answer the same thing: if we buy one more piece of evidence, which decision could it change?

The formal study of this series has already delivered a batch of results. For a patching task on a tool that reads a work log line by line, ten candidates were generated under each of four briefs, and all forty passed the 103 shared checks. No candidate failed on any known check, which is reassuring; but with all four arms, one per brief, at 10/10, the comparison has lost its ability to discriminate. Several proposals follow easily from here: repeat the same task thirty more times, switch to a harder batch of tasks, make the acceptance stricter, or have someone read the programs and record how long the review took. Each proposal costs money, but each buys different evidence and answers a different question.

[The Reliability part](https://medium.com/p/4b6d147bff0d) of my earlier series Green Is Not Done dealt with a neighboring decision: how much evidence a class of tasks has to accumulate before an agent's authorization is widened. That piece cared about whether repeated deliveries all succeed and how much budget to keep for human re-checking; its reading of pass^k is restated with an example in section 4. This series changes the object to a brief: whether it deserves to follow every assignment that comes after. The object changes, but the discipline about denominators matters just as much. Which observations are independent of each other, which are repetitions of the same task, and which actually belong to a different workflow all affect how large a conclusion one number can support.

This piece first pins down what changed this time, then reads the four-arm clean sweep. It then places the same success rate inside two ways of working, to see why the number changes; then, with the overall success rate held equal, it looks at which tasks the failures land on. Those judgments lead to the choice of the next budget, and end in a comparison card that has not yet been executed: it spells out how to assign the work, how to count, how to record human time, and when to adopt.

## 2. First, identify what changed this time: three kinds of variation, three units of comparison

In discussions of evaluation work, "run it again" has at least three meanings. All three produce new candidates, but they hold different conditions fixed and answer different questions.

The first is **repetition under the same conditions**. The model generates another candidate for the same task, using the same brief, model settings and execution policy, letting the parts of the generation process that cannot be pinned down vary on their own. Sending the same parser-patch brief ten times, unchanged, asks how stable this approach is on this one task.

The second is a **document intervention**. The task's requirements, tools, budget and acceptance stay the same; only the brief changes. I call the shared specification P and the version with the self-check added Q; the comparison asks whether attaching this self-check section changes the results. The intervention, here, is the part that is deliberately altered and set up for comparison.

The third is **changing the task or the environment**. For example, carrying this approach over to a CSV patch, or switching to another model. In that case what is held fixed first is the procedure for building the brief and the acceptance principles; the documents for each task still have to be prepared from that task's own requirements, and the parser's sentences cannot be pasted across as they are. The harness, the execution tool that arranges tool use, runs the work and observes the results, may change as well. Comparisons of this kind ask whether the conclusion can travel to other work and other environments; section 9 explains how each task's P and Q are built.

The table below sets the three side by side. In the table, task means the original requirements together with the repo's seed commit; packet is the complete input handed to the model in one run. Byte-identical means that not one byte of the input changed. Read first what each row holds fixed and what it changes, then how far the formal study actually went.

The last column shows that the formal study repeated each of the four packets ten times on the same task, and also arranged document differences between the arms. B is built on the full text of A and adds the concentrated rationale and the six self-check items at the same time, so A versus B cannot separate the two contributions. C adds to B a whole section of optional suggestions on how to arrange the implementation (the optional pattern that section 3 refers to); B versus C can in principle compare that whole increment, but cannot be broken down further into the effect of individual suggestions, word count, or how the model understood the text.

This round's four arms all passed, giving no difference that could support ranking the effects. The study also arranged no comparison across tasks or environments, so the conclusion remains confined to this one patching task.

Counting has to follow the question as well. Forty generations are still forty trials of the same patching task, not forty engineering situations. The 103 checks ask questions of one candidate, for instance whether it handles a wrong type and whether it preserves whitespace; however many checks there are, they do not turn into 103 model deliveries.

The same applies to property-based testing, where a tool automatically produces more inputs. The Hypothesis documentation explains that its generation distribution exists to find bugs, not to simulate real production traffic ([Hypothesis, Domain and distribution](https://hypothesis.readthedocs.io/en/latest/explanation/domain.html)). Having tested ten thousand generated inputs is not the same as ten thousand successes in production.

The structural comparison has another number that is easy to inflate: pairing each arm's ten candidates two at a time gives 45 pairs, 180 across the four arms. The same program recurs in different pairs, so these pairs share the original forty programs and cannot be counted as 180 independent observations.

If the next round lets the agent use tools within a single work session, yet another easily miscounted unit appears. The agent may write a version, run the public checks, see a failure and revise, leaving several intermediate versions inside one session. Those versions are all process records of a single trial; only the candidate frozen when the session ends goes to external acceptance. However many intermediate versions there are, they do not increase the number of independent samples.

The third kind of variation needs its own entry because the environment itself changes results. In one engineering article, Anthropic held the model, the harness and the task fixed and adjusted only the available resources and the kill threshold, the limit at which a run is forcibly stopped, and the results changed with them. In the SWE-bench cross-comparison, the author used 227 tasks with 10 samples each; moving RAM from 1× to 5× gave a gap of 1.54 percentage points, that is, the direct difference between the two success proportions ([Segato, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/infrastructure-noise)). The article does not say clearly whether each resource condition had its own ten samples, so I do not derive a total request count myself.

Another paper on coding agents also shows that when the harness execution policy differs, the effect of raising effort changes with it ([Prompt-Induced Waste in Coding Agents, arXiv 2608.01347v6](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.01347v6), §6.1). Both studies point to the same thing: changing the model, the harness or the resources is a variation of another kind, and a document comparison obtained in the old environment cannot vouch directly for the new one.

These studies cannot be used to infer what caused the variation in this series. The formal study was tool-free, single-shot generation; the model did not install packages, build the project or run tests. The forty candidates differ in structure, but the context the provider actually used and the sampling settings, temperature and random seed, can neither be fixed nor seen; I cannot attribute the differences to RAM or to any setting I cannot observe. The random seed here is the one used for sampling, which is different from the seed commit that fixes the starting code mentioned earlier.

What can be stated clearly is the study's stopping rule: when a generation slot reaches 600 seconds, the termination signal SIGTERM is sent, the run is given at most 5 more seconds, and only if it still has not stopped is it force-ended with SIGKILL. There is also a CLI stop threshold of USD 5 per slot.

The stopping rule itself changes a comparison. One study repeated its measurements on 5 SWE-bench Verified tasks; it originally allowed at most 60 turns, and the terse specification had more runs truncated as a result; after the limit was changed to 120 turns, the researchers reran 112 runs ([Can your AI agent be cheaper?, arXiv 2608.25399v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25399v1), Appendix A). How much work is allowed is part of the method; writing down only the model name does not make two conditions the same.

## 3. Four arms at 10/10: identical observed results do not establish equivalence

Now back to the formal results. The task comes from my `medium-articles` repo: `research/scripts/codex_jsonl.py` reads a JSONL work log, that is, one JSON record per line. Normal content goes to stdout, diagnostics go to stderr, and at the end the exit code reports the outcome of the whole run. The fixed seed commit had two reproducible problems: it crashed when the root-level value was `[]` or `null`; and when a message's `text` was the number `123`, followed by a valid completion, it printed the number as review text and reported success with exit 0.

The four briefs were built on thirteen shared requirements, R01–R13, but their wording is not necessarily synonymous throughout; section 6 returns to the difference in scope over new CLI options. A0, at 936 words, already contains the complete objective; A, at 1,655 words, expands the requirements item by item; B, at 1,923 words, adds rationale and six self-check items after the full text of A; C, at 2,223 words, adds one more section of optional plain-text implementation suggestions. The counts use whitespace-separated words; they include headings and code, exclude the starting code, and are not the token count the model processes.

To check these differences yourself, open the full English text of the four specs: [A0](https://github.com/fantasybz/medium-articles/blob/774759192ab0e0b2da3d8b163eb3f07f3d12f4c3/research/experiments/same-spec-ten-runs/results/formal-2026-09-25/source/specs/A0.md), [A](https://github.com/fantasybz/medium-articles/blob/774759192ab0e0b2da3d8b163eb3f07f3d12f4c3/research/experiments/same-spec-ten-runs/results/formal-2026-09-25/source/specs/A.md), [B](https://github.com/fantasybz/medium-articles/blob/774759192ab0e0b2da3d8b163eb3f07f3d12f4c3/research/experiments/same-spec-ten-runs/results/formal-2026-09-25/source/specs/B.md), [C](https://github.com/fantasybz/medium-articles/blob/774759192ab0e0b2da3d8b163eb3f07f3d12f4c3/research/experiments/same-spec-ten-runs/results/formal-2026-09-25/source/specs/C.md). The [full-text and line-by-line diff guide](https://github.com/fantasybz/medium-articles/blob/774759192ab0e0b2da3d8b163eb3f07f3d12f4c3/research/experiments/same-spec-ten-runs/SPECS.md) also provides the line-by-line comparisons A0 → A, A → B and B → C, so a reader can first confirm what each step changed and then judge which comparison the results can support.

Each arm's ten slots, the ten generations scheduled for it, were fixed in advance, with no retries, repairs, substitutes or samples added afterward. Generation used `claude-opus-5-5` at high effort; the model had no tools and could submit one file per run. Acceptance was run on all forty candidates together, and only after every generation had finished: 102 batch cases read the results after the full input had been sent; 1 further streaming probe deliberately withheld the end-of-input signal, EOF, to see first whether messages could get out. Operational conditions were checked separately, and a candidate counted as accepted only if both the operational conditions and the 103 behavioral checks held.

All forty candidates were accepted.

That is easy to read as "the four briefs are equally good". A purely hypothetical calculation shows the gap: if each run were independent with a 95% success probability, the probability of ten passes in ten is about 59.9%; even at a success probability of only 80%, there is still roughly a 10.7% chance of handing in ten passes out of ten. Approaches that perform differently can leave behind the same 10/10.

Even identical observed results leave uncertainty about the underlying success probability; a statistical interval describes that uncertainty. Under the assumption that each arm's ten observations are independent, made under the same conditions, and share one success probability, the two-sided 95% Clopper–Pearson interval for 10/10 runs from about 69.15% to 100%. It is an exact interval method for a binomial proportion; in this case the lower bound can be computed from one question: how low can the success rate go before the probability of ten passes in ten falls to only 2.5%? Solving p¹⁰ = 0.025 gives about 0.6915.

This is not a guarantee that the next run has at least a 69% chance of success, nor does it mean the success rate has a 95% probability of lying in this already computed interval. What 95% describes is that, in repeated sampling under the assumptions, this interval method covers the true value at least 95% of the time. Identical wide intervals do not establish that the four arms share the same unknown success probability.

If what the team really wants to say is "the gap is small enough to treat as the same", that is an equivalence judgment, and it needs a different kind of evidence. The team first has to set a threshold for how small a difference counts as the same, then estimate an interval for the difference between two arms and confirm that the whole interval falls inside the threshold. The formal study set no equivalence threshold in advance and provides neither the interval for the difference nor the precision planning an equivalence judgment needs. So this data cannot show that the four arms are equivalent, and it cannot show that longer text is useless either.

The conclusion it supports is narrower: on this task and under these conditions, each of the four packets delivered, in all ten of its slots, a candidate that passed the 103 checks, and no failure was observed. That conclusion carries one more condition: 103 is a finite number of checks.

When EvalPlus expanded the tests on 164 HumanEval function tasks across 26 models, it found errors the original tests had not identified, and even changed the ranking between models ([EvalPlus, arXiv 2305.01210v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.01210v3), §3). "No failure observed" covers only what the yardstick, the acceptance checks taken together, can see.

Cost and time are descriptive only. Estimated at CLI list prices, the ten generations of A0, A, B and C cost in total about USD 1.25, 1.38, 1.51 and 1.55 respectively, about 5.69 altogether. That is not a bill, and it excludes research, process pilots, writing and human cost. The median generation times were about 37.3, 37.3, 40.3 and 37.2 seconds; C's longest single run took 59.7 seconds. These are model generation times only, excluding acceptance and human handling.

Cost rises roughly as the document grows. A versus B changes rationale and self-check together, so their contributions cannot be separated; B versus C can compare the whole optional-pattern increment, but this round's cost is still only descriptive: it cannot be used to break down the effect of individual suggestions or word count, and it is not enough to rank delivery quality.

Cost itself also varies across repeated runs: in the SWE-bench study mentioned in the previous section, for repeated runs of the same specification and effort, the researchers summarized the cost dispersion with a geometric standard deviation (geometric SD), with a median of ×1.34; this measure expresses dispersion as a multiplicative factor rather than as a difference of some number of dollars (arXiv 2608.25399v1, §4.3). So looking at how much one run cost cannot say how the cost varies under the same conditions.

More to the point, the human cost that an EM needs most, the time spent reading the program, correcting, approving and maintaining documents, was not measured at all in this round.

So, would repeating the same task a few dozen more times close that gap? Under the same assumption of independent, identically distributed trials, if all n runs succeed, the lower bound of the 95% interval is the n-th root of 0.025: about 88.4% for 30 passes out of 30, about 96.4% for 100 out of 100.

That budget gives us more confidence in one judgment: this packet rarely fails on this one task. If all four arms keep passing, the arms still cannot be told apart, and nothing is learned about another tool or another kind of work. The repetition is directly useful only when what the team cares about is performance on the same frozen patching task, with the same packet and delivery policy. If this parser later gets new requirements or a new seed commit, that is a different task, and the repetitions here cannot stand in for it.

## 4. The same success rate, different usage policies

Suppose an approach succeeds with a probability of 80% on every run. A team that receives three results in a row and adopts each one directly cares whether none of the three went wrong; a team that runs the same task three times and then picks one successful result cares whether at least one was found. Both are called success rates, but they ask different things.

τ-bench uses pass^k for all k runs succeeding and pass@k for at least one success in k independent attempts; both are computed per task first and then averaged across tasks ([Yao et al., arXiv 2406.12045v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.12045v1), §3). The original study uses simulated customer-service tasks in retail and aviation; this piece borrows only the definitions, not its success rates.

What follows is a purely hypothetical example, not an estimate from this round. All four arms of the formal study are 10/10, which allows reporting the observed proportion and an interval under assumptions, but the interval is still wide: it cannot pin the unknown success rate at exactly 100%, and it cannot be used to support the p=0.8 below. Now assume, separately, that each attempt at some fixed task is independent of the others, with a success probability of 0.8, and consider three attempts (k=3). Each row of the table represents one usage policy. The rows that involve picking assume a selection mechanism that recognizes the correct result every time, called the selector; that ideal premise will be taken away and examined shortly. Read the second column, the question each row answers, before the third column, the conditions under which it holds.

📌【在此插入表 table-02.png】

The same p in the second and third rows gives results that differ by nearly a factor of two. The second row describes an unattended situation: whatever the agent hands in is adopted each time, and any single error counts as a failure. That is exactly the situation the Reliability part cares about when it discusses widening authorization. The third row describes a different way of working: the team is willing to pay for three attempts at the same task and lets some mechanism pick the successful one.

The fourth row shows that the selection policy changes cost as well. If success or failure can be judged immediately after each attempt, the probability of succeeding on the first is 0.8, and a third attempt is needed only when the first two both failed; the expected number of attempts is 1 + 0.2 + 0.04 = 1.24. Assuming each generation costs USD 0.10 and each acceptance check USD 0.02, the expected cost of stopping at the first success is about USD 0.1488; running all three every time and then picking is USD 0.36. Both have a success rate of 99.2%, yet the spending differs by more than a factor of two. The following code can be used to check the arithmetic:

```python
# Worked check (purely hypothetical numbers; this piece did not execute this code)
p, k = 0.8, 3
pass_at_k = 1 - (1 - p) ** k                       # 0.992: at least one success in three
pass_pow_k = p ** k                                # 0.512: all three succeed
expected_n = sum((1 - p) ** i for i in range(k))   # 1 + 0.2 + 0.04 = 1.24 attempts
cost_each = 0.10 + 0.02                            # assumed: USD 0.10 generation, USD 0.02 acceptance
print(expected_n * cost_each, k * cost_each)       # about 0.1488 versus 0.36
```

Two qualifications apply to that 99.2% figure. The first comes from the selector. The table assumes it always judges correctly, but a real selector is itself an acceptance yardstick: it can let a wrong candidate through, and it can reject a right one. A wrong candidate that gets through is delivered mixed in with the "successes"; a right candidate wrongly rejected costs the team extra attempts. Part 2, Contract, deals with exactly that question: how reliable the yardstick is.

The second qualification comes from feedback. If the agent sees the previous failure message and then corrects, the later attempt is no longer independent of the earlier one. That is a different policy; it cannot be estimated with the formula above, and can only be measured by treating the whole process as one trial.

When Kapoor and colleagues compare agents, they include a retry baseline, one that only retries without adding any more elaborate strategy, together with cost, reminding readers that "try a few more times" can by itself be a strong competitor ([AI Agents That Matter, arXiv 2407.01502v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502v1), §2.2–2.3). Applied to the decision this piece is about: if Q's advantage is only that the agent did a few more rounds of checking, the team should first ask whether spending the same budget on more retries of P would get similar deliveries. The paper also says plainly that it did not adequately measure human, annotation and maintenance cost; that part still has to be filled in by the team itself.

The policy adopted by the next round's comparison card allows the agent to receive feedback. Each slot is one bounded tool session: the agent may run the public checks and correct after seeing a failure; only after the session ends does external acceptance begin. Every round of correction inside a session is part of the same trial and cannot be counted as the k in pass@k. The comparison records the final results under P and Q and the budget each used, with the same session policy in both conditions.

## 5. Both at 10/20, and it still matters which tasks failed

The previous section assumed a single task. A real comparison usually has more than one, with each task repeated a few times, and that brings out another problem: looking only at the overall success rate throws away the information about where the failures fall.

What follows is again a purely hypothetical example. Two methods, X and Y, each handle the same ten tasks, run twice per task, and both total 10 successes out of 20. The pass^2 and pass@2 in the table describe this batch of observations: the share of tasks that passed both times, and the share that passed at least once; the true success probability of each task cannot be inferred backward from two results. Read the second column first, to confirm how the successes and failures are distributed, then compare the last two columns.

📌【在此插入表 table-03.png】

If only the overall success rate is kept, X and Y both read 50% and look identical. Only after keeping the task ID that says which task each result belongs to can we see two different patterns of observation and ask what to investigate next; the table is still not enough to diagnose either method's failure mechanism.

X's ten failures are concentrated on five tasks. I would first check whether those five share an obligation, a yardstick or a kind of work: whether a requirement is still undecided, whether the basis for the expected result or the comparison procedure (the oracle) wrongly rejects a legitimate way of writing the program, or whether the current approach fails more often on one class of work. With only two results per task, we cannot conclude that their success probability is zero, nor that more retries would be useless. The other five tasks' two results agree, but that only says both runs passed; it is not yet enough to guarantee a later single delivery.

Y shows one pass and one failure on every task. This shows that in this batch, results on the same task were inconsistent, but it does not support assuming that the true success probability per run is exactly one half, or that the attempts are independent of each other. The diagnostic lead it offers is: are selection, re-checking or bounded retries worth including in the next comparison? If the team is going to adopt a process of that kind, the selector's misjudgments and cost have to be evaluated alongside it. X and Y let us see the direction to pursue; they have not answered which cause produced the failures, or which remedy is sure to work.

This difference also affects document comparison. Suppose Q's overall success rate is only slightly higher than P's: it could be that a few tasks showed much better observed results while a few others got worse, or that the improvement is spread across many tasks. Only by keeping each task's results under P and under Q is there a chance to tell these patterns apart, trace the obligations that regressed, and estimate the differences between tasks. Even if every task's observations point the same way, sampling uncertainty and task representativeness still have to be dealt with before discussing whether the self-check has wider use.

So the records have to keep identity from the start. `task_id` corresponds to the original requirements and the repo's seed commit; every trial under the same task must map back to its condition and to which repetition it is. In analysis, results on the same task are treated as one group rather than pulled apart into unrelated data; this preserves task clustering, and the analysis then allows repeated results on the same task to be dependent: they share one set of requirements, one starting point, and may get stuck on the same hard spot. Ignoring that dependence can make an interval look too narrow. τ-bench first keeps the success count of each task and then averages across tasks; that way of computing preserves the structure at the task level.

## 6. Work backward from the question to the next piece of evidence

The preceding sections each dealt with one misreading. Put together, they answer the opening question: where should the next budget go? My approach is to first identify which kind of uncertainty we are stuck on, then choose the evidence that resolves it. The table below collects six common situations. In it, incoming means the work that actually comes in; AST is the syntactic structure extracted from code, which section 7 explains further. Find your situation in the first two columns, then the evidence to add in the third; the fourth lists the moves most often substituted for it that cannot answer the question.

📌【在此插入表 table-04.png】

**Not enough data.** When the interval crosses the threshold, the most immediate reaction is usually "run a little more". First separate the goals: is it to estimate how stable one fixed task is, or the average effect over a class of work? Then assess how task differences and same-task dependence affect precision. The X/Y example in the previous section had only two observations per task; it can hint at what to check, but cannot tell us directly which way the budget should move. If independent pilot data and reasonable assumptions show large differences between tasks, consider adding tasks; if the question is to see the fluctuation within a task, consider adding repetitions. Either arrangement still has to be set against the same goal, comparing the required cost and the achievable precision in advance. The rules for adding and stopping also have to be decided before the formal results are seen; one cannot notice which result is approaching what one hoped for and keep adding until it holds.

"Undetermined" is also a legitimate result. Skill Issue used Claude Code with Sonnet 4.6 to optimize SKILL documents on three Kotlin repos; the tasks held back until evaluation numbered 20, 26 and 23 respectively ([Skill Issue, arXiv 2609.12742v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.12742v1), §3–4.2). The solved counts went from 13 to 16, 16 to 20 and 14 to 15, which all look like increases.

The study also computed a paired composite score, comparing the rewritten version with the original on the same tasks; against a self-comparison baseline of 0.5, the three repos scored .554, .567 and .525, about 4.9 percentage points higher on average. This is not a solve rate; it also folds in factors such as test tampering, success claims and hidden tests. The authors used a sign test to check the direction of the paired differences; none of the optimizations reached the .05 significance threshold, with the lowest p-value at .29.

This cannot show that SKILL documents are ineffective, and p=.29 cannot be read as "a 29% chance the document is useless". At this amount of data, the gain still cannot be separated from the natural variation caused by rerunning. A document change in practice will very likely stop in the same place: an observed improvement, with evidence not yet sufficient for an adoption judgment.

**Hitting the ceiling (observations cannot discriminate).** The formal study is exactly this situation. Section 3 already worked it out: if the same task keeps being repeated and every arm still passes in full, this task's interval lower bound rises gradually, while there is still no observed difference between arms. New trials might also fail, so one cannot assert in advance that adding runs will keep the clean sweep. An extension experiment in another study showed the same thing: 5 tasks, 5 conditions, each condition repeated 3 times, 75 runs in total, and every condition at 15/15 (arXiv 2608.01347v6; in that setting, the extension experiment measured the effect of increasing effort). In observations where the success rates are all saturated, there is no success difference available to tell the conditions apart.

At this point "switch to harder tasks" is only half right. The tasks have to be able to produce differences, and they also have to represent the work on which the team actually intends to adopt the approach; hard tasks picked specially to widen the gap yield conclusions that apply only to those hard tasks. The other option is to look at outcomes other than the success rate, such as the kinds of failure or the human effort. Those outcomes have to be fixed before the comparison; one cannot switch to a new metric on the spot after seeing the success rates tie.

**Requirements differ between conditions.** If the conditions carry a difference beyond the component being compared, no number of samples can separate the effects. The formal study has one such spot: on new CLI arguments, A0 says "No new CLI arguments or external dependencies are required", while A goes further in R13 and excludes them, listing "adding CLI options" among the things that are "outside this task"; B and C carry A's text. The formal grader, the scoring program, did not fully check extra CLI options or the `main` interface, so what effect this difference had remains unknown. A frozen packet cannot be modified after the fact; adding samples for it would only reproduce the same extra difference again and again. If the next round is to compare, align the requirements first, then regenerate.

The design of P and Q follows the same principle: each task's Q begins with the full text of the shared P and appends only the self-check section approved under the policy fixed in advance. The parser example can use the original six questions from frozen B; other tasks must first map each self-check question onto their own existing requirements, a step called mapping, and cannot copy across obligations such as EOF and tokens that do not apply. Section 9 explains the mapping policy. That Q preserves P in full can be checked byte by byte, but whether a requirement has been slipped in semantically still needs a separate review.

**The task does not represent the target work.** One parser can demonstrate the method, but it cannot represent a team's everyday patching. If the conclusion is to apply to "patches to Python tools", the tasks have to be drawn from the actual sources of that work. Section 9 explains how to build that list.

**The yardstick cannot see.** If the problem lies in acceptance, as in the Overview's CSV case where the contract allows either LF or CRLF as a record terminator but the byte-by-byte comparison accepts only one of them and wrongly rejects legitimate output, then generating more only produces more misjudged candidates. The next step should be to fix the oracle, keep the old version and the original scoring records, and re-score the same existing candidates consistently. That step needs no new model generation.

**Human cost unknown.** This is the question an EM asks most often, and the one most often replaced by machine metrics. AST similarity describes what the structure of a program looks like, and model list prices describe the cost of generation; neither can say how much effort it takes a person to understand and take over the program. To answer the human question, arrange for a person to take over and leave records. Section 10 explains what to record.

## 7. Two candidates that both passed: which review action does the difference point to?

Picture two programs laid out in front of you: both pass, but their functions are arranged differently. A computer can organize a program into an abstract syntax tree (AST), which keeps structure such as "this is a conditional, that is a function call", and then compute a content fingerprint, a hash, from that representation. Any difference retained in the representation can produce a different hash.

This study first unified some identifier names, a step called normalization, and then computed the AST hash; all forty differ. However, this transformation keeps attribute names, import paths, constants and program order, and it does not track which binding a variable refers to in different scopes (scope binding). So a different hash only means the transformed representations differ; it does not guarantee that the control flow differs, still less that quality is worse. The full descriptive statistics are in the [formal study results](https://github.com/fantasybz/medium-articles/blob/774759192ab0e0b2da3d8b163eb3f07f3d12f4c3/research/experiments/same-spec-ten-runs/RESULTS.md).

For a reviewer, the next step is to trace one requirement through the implementation and ask: where does this difference oblige me to look more closely? Below, a static reading of two candidates shows how that question is asked.

For this static reading, done after the study was complete, the two A0 candidates that come earliest in the frozen execution order were selected before their code was read: [formal-01-A0](https://github.com/fantasybz/medium-articles/blob/774759192ab0e0b2da3d8b163eb3f07f3d12f4c3/research/experiments/same-spec-ten-runs/results/formal-2026-09-25/formal-01-A0/candidate/codex_jsonl.py) and [formal-02-A0](https://github.com/fantasybz/medium-articles/blob/774759192ab0e0b2da3d8b163eb3f07f3d12f4c3/research/experiments/same-spec-ten-runs/results/formal-2026-09-25/formal-02-A0/candidate/codex_jsonl.py). The selection did not consult scores or the degree of difference; still, this is a teaching choice made after the fact, not an analysis planned before the study, and not a representative sample. Both candidates passed the 103 checks. What follows is static reading only; nobody's time to understand or modify the programs was measured.

Before opening the programs, know what to look for. R05 is the requirement number for the message field: when `text` is missing, `null` or the empty string, it means there is no message: it produces no output and does not count as a spoken message; a nonempty string is "printed unchanged", sent to the caller immediately (flushed), and whitespace-only strings "remain valid". Every other non-string value, including `false`, `0`, arrays and objects, counts as a format error, called malformed. R03 then requires exactly one malformed-event diagnostic on stderr per malformed line, and that the parser "continue reading" the input that follows; a malformed `turn.failed` also emits its legacy failure diagnostic under R10.

Only at the end is the overall result decided. R11 asks, in order, at EOF: is there any explicit failure? If so, return 3; otherwise, is there any malformed event? If so, return 6; then check in turn for 4, no valid completed turn, and 5, completed without a spoken message, and return 0 only if none applies. Once an earlier item holds, a later one is not allowed the final say, regardless of the size of the numbers. R12 requires that only 3, 4 and 5 carry the legacy end summary.

Let me demonstrate one derivation first. The input has two lines: the first is an agent message whose `text` is `false`; the second is a valid completion event. `false` is not a string, so it is malformed: the first line produces exactly one diagnostic, emits nothing on stdout, and counts as no message. The second line records a valid completion. At EOF there is no explicit failure but there is a malformed event, so the result is 6, with no end summary.

Now derive one yourself. The first line of input is an agent message whose `text` is the number `123`; the second is a well-formed `turn.failed` event; then the input ends, with no completion event. Under R10, a `turn.failed` event "always sets explicit failure". At EOF, three conditions hold at once: explicit failure, malformed, and no valid completion; by precedence, explicit failure wins, so the result is 3, with the end summary attached. This input is worth deriving because it triggers three conditions at the same time; a single mistake in the order of the EOF decision can turn the result into 6 or 4.

With the expected results in hand, read the two programs with three review questions. The table below marks where each implementation answers them. Compare across each row: for the same obligation, where do you have to look in each program? In the table, dict is a Python dictionary that stores data; a handler is the function that processes one class of event; and `_State` is the name of the class the second program uses to hold its state.

The first row is the one to pause on. `formal-02-A0` handles null and the empty string first, then rejects non-strings. There is nothing wrong with that order in itself, but it makes the first check the critical one: if that guard were written as a truthiness test, `if not text: return`, deciding only whether the value counts as true or false, it would send every value that "looks empty" down the same silent early return, so `false` and `0` would be treated as no message and skipped without a diagnostic. Three review actions follow from this reading. First, approval has to be given to a specific candidate. Even when two programs pass the same checks, the approval of the first cannot be transferred to the second; with a different candidate, the implementation path for the same obligation has to be found again.

Second, the contract allows this freedom of implementation. The choice made in R05 might be different in another product; here the frozen contract settled the R05 policy, and acceptance checked that behavior alongside the existing compatibility behaviors the other clauses require to be preserved. As long as the behavior meets the contract, a dict and a class are both legitimate variation, and "it doesn't look like the last one" cannot be a reason to reject.

Third, if the team wants to settle on one shared architecture on the grounds of lowering the cost of understanding or modification, it has to design a separate comparison; the compatibility and architectural obligations already in force are still enforced by whoever holds the authority and responsibility for them. The comparison could be arranged like this: give both candidates the same already-decided modification task, observe the time different people taking over need to understand the requirements, complete the modification and pass acceptance, and keep the failures. This reading did not do that, so the table above cannot be used to estimate "a few fewer places to look" or "a few minutes faster".

Both original files are identified by their SHA-256 hashes: `formal-01-A0` is `7cc9bf5a24969310f18a50aad43ac26d4a59066321b2256ec03806da076dd2b2`, and `formal-02-A0` is `f2a65e9e26aaa195ab1c0b8caf2c1920cb4e3d2ee3ad2abf30d1620a78008a57`. The AST node-type cosine for this pair is about 0.994. It first counts how many times each kind of syntax node appears, then compares the direction of those count distributions, without preserving node order. Even when two programs use similar numbers of conditionals and calls, that does not mean the conditionals sit in the same places or that the execution paths are the same. So a close value cannot replace the obligation-by-obligation reading just done.

## 8. Keeping the old results: what to recheck when the yardstick, the program or the method changes

All four arms at 10/10 cannot rank the documents, but the batch is still useful. An Anthropic engineering article on agent evals separates capability evals from regression evals: a capability set has to be able to show improvement, while a regression set guards against existing abilities slipping. One sentence in it is direct: "An eval at 100% tracks regressions but provides no signal for improvement." ([Demystifying evals for AI agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents), Capability vs. regression evals; Step 7). This is a vendor's engineering-practice advice, not a controlled experiment; but it gives 40/40 its next use: organize the batch into a regression asset, something used, when a change comes later, to confirm that what used to work has not regressed.

When organizing a regression asset, distinguish three actions. They hold different things fixed and answer different questions.

The first action checks the grader, the scoring program. First fix a set of test materials (fixtures) whose behavior has been verified: positive controls that should be accepted, and negative controls that should be rejected; then swap in the new grader and confirm that the new yardstick neither wrongly rejects legitimate behavior nor lets known faulty versions through. The forty passing candidates can serve as positive controls for the behaviors already verified; they are not implementations proven to satisfy the whole contract. When building a fixture for one grader assertion, the behavior that assertion needs still has to be verified first, and positive controls alone are not enough: known faulty versions are needed too.

The teaching workbench built afterward supplied both kinds of control: one legitimate output that rewrites only the wording of the malformed-diagnostic reason should be accepted, and six deliberately faulty versions all received the expected verdict. These fixtures are teaching devices; they are not part of the formal study's 103 checks.

The second action checks a new candidate. Fix the same contract's 102 batch cases and the streaming probe, and use them to check every later patch to `codex_jsonl.py`, confirming that it still satisfies the covered requirements. This layer answers whether this new artifact has regressed, regardless of whether a person or an agent wrote it.

The third action checks the delivery method. After the model, the harness or the tool-use policy changes, new generations have to be obtained, and their distribution on the applicable tasks kept together with the policy version, before one can judge whether the new method has regressed. Re-scoring the old forty candidates with a new grader only updates the judgment on that old batch of artifacts; it checks no new candidate and cannot replace new generation.

Saving another copy, renaming the file, or rewriting the same task once does not turn a task whose answer has been seen back into a new one.

To compare documents or evaluate a new capability, set up separate formal tasks and scoring assets, and isolate them while the documents are being tuned. Nor can the results of a formal comparison be turned around to modify the same Q and then claim improvement on the same batch of tasks. ## 9. The next comparison card: how to assign work, how to count, where to draw tasks from (not yet executed)

If the first two layers have been handled and the team still wants to know whether an added optional self-check deserves to become the default, a new comparison is needed. Below is the comparison card I designed for that question. **This is a design, not yet executed; the mapping and archiving for the formal tasks are not yet complete either.** It plans bounded agent sessions with tool access, a different process from the forty tool-free, single-shot generations of the formal study, and it cannot be recast after the fact as a continuation of that round. The full fields and a blank template are in the [comparison plan example](https://github.com/fantasybz/medium-articles/blob/774759192ab0e0b2da3d8b163eb3f07f3d12f4c3/research/2026-10/same-spec-ten-runs/rebuild-2026-09-28/comparison-plan-example.md); the Overview already excerpted the main fields of the [document-change evaluation proposal](https://github.com/fantasybz/medium-articles/blob/774759192ab0e0b2da3d8b163eb3f07f3d12f4c3/research/2026-10/same-spec-ten-runs/rebuild-2026-09-28/document-change-proposal.md). This piece explains only three things on the card: the policy, the denominator and the source of tasks.

**Fix the policy first.** The comparison has to stay close to the workflow the team intends to adopt, so the card first writes down how one unit of work proceeds. For each scheduled slot, a fresh session starts from a repo rebuilt to the designated starting point, and the agent works within the tools, permissions and budget fixed in advance. The agent may read the repo, run the public checks, and correct the program within the same session. When the session ends, the final candidate is frozen first; then an external program runs the acceptance the agent cannot reach. The acceptance result is not fed back into that session; on failure, no new session is opened as a substitute. For the same task, P and Q differ only in the approved self-check section; all other requirements, starting code, tool settings and budget are the same.

**Across tasks, fix the rewriting policy first.** The parser's own spec change card, the single-task version from Part 1, Specification, uses the original six questions from frozen B, which lets a reader see one concrete intervention clearly; but the six questions involve EOF, token counts and the top-level `error` event, and cannot be pasted straight into every Python patch. This design instead compares a [self-check rewriting policy](https://github.com/fantasybz/medium-articles/blob/774759192ab0e0b2da3d8b163eb3f07f3d12f4c3/research/2026-10/same-spec-ten-runs/rebuild-2026-09-28/self-check-transfer-policy.md) fixed in advance: retain the six checklist positions, and rewrite the questions from the existing clauses of each task's shared specification P. The rewriter may use only P and the public starting-repo information available under both conditions.

Sub-items with no corresponding obligation are not turned into questions; when an entire position has no corresponding obligation and the owner confirms it is not needed this time, keep "not applicable" with the reason, rather than inventing a new requirement to fill out the six. Each task records its number of applicable checklist positions; the six positions cannot be treated as six identical requirements. An undecided policy goes to the owner first, is added back into the shared P, and only then are P and Q built; the answer cannot be supplied in Q alone.

For every task, the complete P and Q, the diff, the clause mapping and the not-applicable reasons must be checked, and the version and content hashes must be archived before any trial. Repetitions of the same task under the same condition use byte-identical packets; the text may differ across tasks, but the procedure for building and reviewing must be the same. Q is not tuned using a hidden grader, reference patches, formal results or grader feedback.

Nor may a task from a process pilot be used to tune Q and then be listed in the formal comparison. A pilot exists to find process problems first; once its answers have been seen to adjust the approach, it has to be kept apart from the formal tasks. If a diagnostic pilot prompts a revision of the general template, archive the new version first and then build a separate, isolated formal set. The effect obtained this way belongs to the whole rewriting-and-review policy and cannot be attributed to any one sentence of frozen B.

The mapping of all target tasks is not yet complete; the comparison card therefore remains a design still to be filled in, and the 288 slots cannot be started directly.

"The same tools" here means the same available tools, permissions and budget; it does not require P and Q to produce identical sequences of tool calls. If the self-check built under the policy leads the agent to run the public checks a few more times, that may well be exactly the way it works, so it should be recorded rather than ruled out in advance. But the trace that records the operations can only tell us what the agent did; it cannot let us read why it did so.

**Then fix the denominator.** Suppose one patching task is done three times under P and three under Q: that is one task and six scheduled units of work, each with its own session. Five revisions inside one session still occupy one unit of work. The `task_id`, `work_id` and `session_id` below record those three layers of identity; the fields have not yet been filled with data.

```yaml
# Trial record fields (design specification, not yet executed; field sketch, no data)
task_id: the original requirements and the repo's starting commit
work_id: one policy trial scheduled in advance (linked to task_id, P/Q, repetition number, block and date)
session_id: the execution session for this slot; exactly one per work_id, no substitute opened
candidate_sha256: the final candidate frozen when the session ends; intermediate versions stay in the trace only
operational_ok: whether tools, permissions, budget and stop conditions met the rules fixed in advance
external_acceptance: the hidden acceptance result and the grader version
primary_outcome: operational_ok and external_acceptance both hold
status: completed / timed out / candidate missing / not started / aborted, with the reason
human_handoff: human handling and final delivery recorded separately (see section 10); not written back into primary_outcome
```

The denominator of the primary outcome is the work_ids scheduled in advance. It is neither the number of intermediate files the agent produced nor the number of units of work finally delivered successfully. When a run times out without completing, the candidate is missing, or the operational conditions are not met, the record is kept and counted as a failure. If overall budget or environment problems leave some slots unstarted, mark their status separately and keep the original schedule; suspend the adoption decision in that case, and do not claim the comparison was completed at its original scale. If the grader hits a fatal error, pause the whole round; after the fix, set up a new grader version and a re-scoring record.

**Assignment and environment.** If every P runs on Monday and every Q on Wednesday, any change in the model service or machine state between those two days can be misread as a document difference. The NIST engineering statistics handbook recommends grouping known, controllable nuisance factors into blocks (blocking), arranging the conditions to be compared within each block, and relying on randomization for the other factors, to reduce the chance that they get mixed up with the conditions ([NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, §5.3.3.2](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pri/section3/pri332.htm)). So the comparison card forms blocks by task and execution day, randomizes the order of P and Q within each block, and records concurrency and the actual start and end times.

The formal study likewise ran four generations in parallel and was scheduled in advance into 10 blocks with the order shuffled within each; but the actual execution windows overlapped rather than forming synchronized, paired time windows, so it cannot be claimed in hindsight to have controlled for time.

Once the agent can use tools, the environment's influence becomes more direct, so resource allocation, the kill threshold, and the model and harness versions all have to be recorded before execution; sampling settings the provider does not open to control are listed as conditions that cannot be fixed.

**Where the tasks come from.** First list all the work that actually came in during a fixed period and falls within scope, then select tasks from it; this complete list available for selection is the sampling frame. The scope in this case is patches in Python tool repos whose behavior and compatibility responsibilities can be clearly agreed. The list has to include work that is not yet finished, cannot be accepted for now, or was later abandoned, and record each item's source, status, and the reason for inclusion or exclusion. Work whose specification is undecided, or for which fair acceptance cannot yet be built, stays on the list too; it may need to go to the owner for clarification first. If in the end only the subset that can be clearly accepted is compared, the conclusion applies only to that subset; the work that was not observed cannot be restored by weighting.

Tasks can be stratified by kind of work, external dependencies, state and streaming, and density of exceptions; if a rare type is deliberately oversampled, the stratified results and the weights chosen in advance have to be reported separately.

Before entering the formal comparison, each task must also complete the confirmation of its shared P, the self-check mapping, the P/Q review and the archiving. When the mapping runs into undecided behavior, go back to the shared requirements; an item cannot be retroactively declared not applicable after a formal failure has been seen.

If only merged historical patches are at hand, set up a separate backtest: use the pre-patch commit as the starting point, isolate the known patch, the answers and the grader in an environment the agent cannot reach, and let the conclusion apply only to this batch of backtest tasks. A task whose answer has been seen, even rewritten, is still not a new holdout task.

How tasks are selected decides where the conclusion generalizes. In its February 2026 research update, METR expanded the study to 57 developers, 143 repos and more than 800 tasks; but the authors themselves point out that which developers and tasks entered the study, changes in compensation, and the attribution of hours when agents run in parallel all prevent them from reliably estimating the current effect ([METR, 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). That study measures productivity, not document effect. Its reminder is that even when task assignment has been randomized, the screening at the very front, which work enters the study, still changes the scope to which the results apply. That is also why the comparison card insists on keeping the complete list and the reasons for exclusion.

**Keep pilot tasks apart from formal tasks.** A diagnostic pilot uses tasks that will not enter the formal set, to check that the reference implementation, the positive and negative controls, the grader and the execution script (runner) behave. A pilot may deliberately cover boundary cases and known faulty versions, because its purpose is to find gaps in the yardstick and the process. But the frequency of those deliberately chosen faulty versions cannot be taken as the failure rate of everyday work, and cannot be used to estimate the formal comparison's ability to recognize the specified difference, called statistical power. While the documents are being tuned, the formal tasks and scoring assets stay isolated.

## 10. Human takeover and scale: what 288 slots cannot buy

The comparison card's primary outcome answers only what the agent delivered. What the EM still has to pay for is the time of the person who takes over. This column is the hardest to record and the most often left out.

**Separate columns for the primary outcome and human remediation.** After each work_id's session ends, the human review, the necessary verification, the bounded fixes and the final delivery are all recorded in full; the ceiling on fixes is decided by the owner in advance. A human modification produces a new candidate hash, linked back to the original one. Even if the final delivery succeeds, the original session's primary outcome does not change. So the records hold two proportions: the pass rate of the agent's original candidates, and the final delivery rate after human handling. Keeping the two apart is what stops human remediation from masking the method's own failures.

**How to record time.** Human time is recorded separately for self-check mapping and verification, reading code, correcting, approving, document revision and maintenance, and passive waiting, with the start, end and interruption rules of each segment defined in advance. While waiting for the agent, if the person is doing other work at the same time, the elapsed time from start to finish (wall-clock) can no longer be counted as human effort.

In its July 2025 randomized controlled study, METR defined "done" as the developer believing the work would pass review, including style, tests and documentation ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)). That result reflects the tooling of early 2025, and the page has been marked as outdated. The 2026 update mentioned in the previous section explains that when agents work in parallel, attributing human working time is itself difficult.

This comparison card also defines its completion point first, but does not adopt METR's endpoint: when the session closes, the original candidate is preserved first; the takeover stage then goes on to record review, bounded fixes, approval and final delivery. Each stage records separately how many minutes of active effort went in and how long elapsed in total; the clock cannot stop at "ready for review" and leave out the cost of the takeover that follows.

When computing human effort per final delivery that meets the agreed requirements, a qualifying delivery, the numerator includes all the time already spent on all scheduled work, including verification, failure handling and bounded fixes; failed work and work with no delivery count too. The denominator is the number of final qualifying deliveries; when there is none, this ratio is not computed.

For example, two scheduled units of work took 6 and 4 minutes respectively, and in the end only one qualifying delivery came out; assuming for now that there are no other shared costs, the effort per qualifying delivery is (6 + 4) ÷ 1 = 10 minutes, not just the time of the one that succeeded. The average per scheduled unit of work is 5 minutes.

Also keep the average human effort per scheduled unit of work, so that a single ratio does not hide the total workload. How shared preparation, self-check mapping and maintenance costs are apportioned also has to be defined before the analysis; they occur before the model runs, which does not mean they can be omitted.

**Sample the deep reads in advance.** Not every candidate needs a deep read, but the sampling rule has to be set before the results are seen: first list the task, the P/Q condition, and the outcome class such as pass or fail as strata, and set how each is sampled; this is stratified sampling. After the acceptance results are in, draw according to the procedure, and keep the sampling probabilities and the record of what is missing.

Reviewer assignment and reading order are also arranged in advance; P/Q labels that can be masked are masked. Code style itself may also give away the condition, and that limit on blinding is recorded as it is. If the same person has already read another implementation of the same task, the second read may be faster because the task is more familiar; that learning effect must not be miscounted as the document's effect. The analysis keeps track of which data came from the same reviewer and which from the same task; observations that share that experience cannot be treated as unrelated.

**The thresholds are examples, not standards.** The comparison card uses two thresholds to show how to read the results: Q at least 5 percentage points above P, for instance from 80% to 85%; and the added human time per qualifying delivery with an interval upper bound of no more than 2 minutes. Both numbers are assumed for the sake of explanation and are not industry standards. A team should set its thresholds from its own work, losses and budget, and write down the reasons: for instance, how much effort it takes to maintain a self-check section, and how much rework each non-qualifying delivery causes on average; those are what a threshold rests on.

**288 slots may not reveal the difference you want to see.** The card's budget draft is 48 tasks × 2 conditions × 3 repetitions, 288 scheduled slots in total. That number says how much the team is willing to spend; it does not say how small a difference it can resolve.

What follows is a rough hypothetical calculation, used only to show the distance between scale and precision; it is not a power analysis for this design. Assume P and Q both succeed around 80% of the time. If each arm's 144 trials are treated as independent of each other, the half-width of the 95% interval for Q−P, the distance from the center of the interval to one side, is about 1.96 × √(2 × 0.8 × 0.2 ÷ 144), roughly 9.2 percentage points.

If the three results on the same task are almost always the same, the effective information is closer to 48 tasks per arm, and the half-width widens to about 16 percentage points. Pairing P and Q on the same batch of tasks can cancel part of the variation caused by task difficulty, so the actual interval may be narrower; how much narrower depends on how consistent the improvement is across tasks.

Compared with the 5-percentage-point threshold, these two rough half-widths point to a result that should be known in advance: if the true improvement is only five or six percentage points, this scale will very likely come back "undetermined". That is not a failure of the design; the team should treat it as a possible outcome before executing.

The team can accept that possibility, or re-evaluate the number of tasks and repetitions from reasonable assumptions about the target effect, task differences and same-task dependence; or re-examine the practical meaning of the threshold, or even hold off on the comparison for now and run a bounded trial under the existing human approval. Whichever is chosen, it is decided before the results are seen. Before formal execution, the calculation still has to be tried under several sets of reasonable assumptions about baseline performance, effect size and within-task dependence, to confirm how much the conclusion depends on those assumptions; this step is called sensitivity analysis.

## 11. The decision record: joint conditions, and the way out when data falls short

The comparison card records how the evidence is obtained; the decision record states who makes which decision, under what conditions, once the evidence is back. The two records are kept separately; otherwise it is all too easy for a team to go back and adjust what counts as success after seeing the results. The decision rules are archived before execution. The formal study made a local archive before execution, but that is not preregistration, where a third party holds the study plan before execution, and some operating-system information was added only after the start. To make the next round's decision rules more credible, I recommend that someone other than the owner responsible for the decision keep the archived version.

**The conditions adoption has to satisfy at the same time.** To adopt the optional self-check as the default on the grounds of improved delivery, look at the effect first. Write the estimated interval for Q−P as [L, U], where L is the lower bound and U the upper. Using the earlier 5-percentage-point example: only when the whole interval lies above 5 does it support an improvement beyond the threshold; when the whole interval lies below 5, an improvement reaching the threshold is ruled out; touching or crossing 5 is undetermined. Section 9 of the Overview lists complete examples of hypothetical intervals.

Beyond the effect, look at human cost, other costs and the necessary conditions at the same time. The upper bound of the interval for added human time per qualifying delivery may not exceed the ceiling set in advance; model cost and document maintenance count too. Method-level conditions such as no overstepping of authority, acceptance not having been altered, and a bearable remediation burden are decided by the owner before the results are seen, and cannot be offset by an average improvement. Required behaviors and compatibility responsibilities already decided are carried out as originally required and are not governed by the effect threshold for the optional self-check.

Practice this judgment once with three hypothetical intervals for human time. If the upper bound of the added time is 1.8 minutes, it meets this example's "no more than 2 minutes"; at an upper bound of 2.4 minutes, we can only say that compliance is not yet confirmed, and cannot conclude from the upper bound alone that human cost has exceeded the limit; only when even the lower bound is above 2 minutes does it support the burden exceeding the ceiling. These are reading examples only, not times that have been measured.

These conditions cannot substitute for one another. If the effect meets the bar but the lower bound of the human-time interval already exceeds the ceiling, the conclusion is "not adopted for now", which cannot be restated as the document having no improving effect. When the effect is still undetermined, "the human cost looks fine" is no reason to flip the verdict to adopt.

Also judge separately whether a candidate meets the required conditions and whether the method reaches the adoption threshold. If a single candidate violates an agreed requirement such as preserving order or keeping duplicate rows, reject that candidate; scores on other items cannot offset it. The method comparison, meanwhile, keeps every trial's pass and failure as they were, and no "observed zero failures" threshold may be added afterward.

**Without human data, whether the investment is worth it cannot be computed.** This round has only generation cost estimated at CLI list prices, and no human time at all. Without the time for reading code, correcting, approving and maintaining, the return on investment (ROI) cannot be computed; AST similarity and review by an AI agent cannot fill that gap either. A comparison lacking human data, however good the effect looks, cannot satisfy the joint conditions.

Insufficient data does not mean standing still. Section 10 of the Overview distinguishes a diagnostic pilot that walks through the process first, a deployment trial run on a small scope of real work, and default adoption. Before the human data is in, the team can run a bounded deployment trial: keep the existing human approval, attach the applicable self-check under the same pre-set policy only to work within a designated scope, write down the owner, the stop conditions and the rollback path in advance, and record what the reviewer actually did extra.

This arrangement can be withdrawn, and it lets the later comparison test its recording process first, for instance how time is segmented and how deep reads are sampled. It is not a randomized comparison, and its results cannot be taken as evidence that the document effect has been established. Its value is that the team can begin accumulating observations from real work without changing authorization.

The first row decides which assets to keep, an action that needs no new generation; it does not mean the evidence for all three regression layers is already in hand. To check a new candidate, that candidate still has to actually go through acceptance; to check the delivery method of a model or harness, new generations still have to be obtained according to the pre-set tasks and trial distribution.

The second row's verdict is "cannot be decided yet": the formal study provides no comparison that could distinguish the self-check's effect, and no human data. Keeping the status quo is a reasoned decision here, and so is a bounded trial under the existing approval; neither needs to claim that the self-check already works.

The third row uses the Overview's hypothetical CSV case to show how the same judgment applies to another kind of work. Suppose the owner has decided that a null date is output as an empty field, and QA has already replaced the byte-by-byte comparison with parsing first and then checking the header row and the complete, ordered data records. Afterward, a candidate removes duplicate rows on its own initiative and is rejected at acceptance. Someone then proposes: "Since it can go wrong, add the self-check to the brief of every export patch."

First sort out which parts have already been handled. Preserving order and keeping duplicate rows are behaviors the caller already required; they belong to the contract. Whether or not the self-check is adopted, every candidate must do this, and a violation is rejected. This layer is already handled by acceptance. The deduplication itself, as an output difference, cannot say whether the model missed the requirement, misunderstood it, or chose the wrong helper function; still less can it show that adding a self-check section would improve the results.

What remains of the proposal is the method-level question this piece deals with, so return to the table in section 6. The present situation belongs at once to "not enough data", "the task does not represent the target work" and "human cost unknown": there is only one hypothetical tool and one rejected candidate; no list of export-type work, and no human records at all. The same-task repetition of section 3 cannot by itself fill the gap in cross-task representativeness and human data. What is needed is a list of incoming export-related work, a P/Q comparison under the same tools and budget, and the reviewer's takeover records.

One thing is easy to copy across by mistake: this tool only has to hand over a complete CSV file; it is not required to output records in real time one by one. So this comparison should not adopt the parser's pre-EOF streaming probe. The self-check has to respect the same line: the parser's first question cannot be pasted into Q as it is, gratuitously adding a streaming obligation; positions with no corresponding promise are marked not applicable under the policy. A self-check about preserving row order and duplicate rows, on the other hand, can link back to the requirements already established in the shared P.

Until then, the decision the team can make is clear. If the owner is willing, confirm each task's shared P and self-check mapping first, attach the applicable self-check to a few export patches, keep the existing human approval, write down the stop conditions, and record the time the reviewer spends. The purpose of that record is to give the later comparison a usable process, not to prove that the self-check works.

**Withdrawal and re-examination.** After adoption as the default, the triggers for re-examination also go into the record. When the requirements have been filled in, or acceptance can now catch a certain kind of faulty version, it is worth re-examining. But acceptance catching an error only shows that it has the ability to detect it; it does not automatically make a preventive self-check redundant. The self-check may still reduce failures, rework and the effort required for human takeover; before removing it, confirm that those burdens remain acceptable after removal. Only when the self-check conflicts with the task requirements, when the comparison has ruled out an improvement worth the investment, or when human cost exceeds the ceiling set in advance, is it withdrawn or its scope narrowed on the corresponding grounds, with the version at withdrawal recorded.

## 12. Closing: after all ten passed

Back to the opening question: all four arms passed ten out of ten, so where should the next budget go?

Looking at this batch of results, the most natural proposal is to run a little more. Having read the preceding sections, I would first ask which decision this budget is meant to change. If the aim is to estimate the success rate of the same frozen patching task under a fixed delivery policy, same-task repetition can add information; if later runs keep passing, the interval lower bound given by that all-success formula from earlier keeps rising; if a failure appears, recompute from the new success count. If the decision is whether a brief should accompany every Python tool patch, same-task repetition cannot answer on its own; what is needed then is tasks that represent that kind of work, records that keep the task ID, and the time a real person spends taking over.

So my position is conditional. If I were the eval owner, and the decision was about a cross-task default, then faced with four arms at 10/10 I would first organize this batch into assets by the three regression uses, marked as disclosed during research and writing and no longer this process's unseen holdout. Method-level regression still needs new generation; replaying old candidates is not enough. Then I would put the budget where it can change a decision: if the yardstick is in doubt, fix the yardstick first; if the task scope is unclear, build the incoming list; if human cost is the question, arrange to observe a real person taking the work over.

If I were the EM, I would require every proposal that adds an optional self-check on the grounds of improved delivery to first spell out the rewriting policy, the denominator, the thresholds and the way human time is recorded. Required obligations are still carried out according to the requirements. Until the comparison results are back and the effect, the human cost and the other pre-set conditions hold at the same time, I would keep the status quo, or run a bounded trial under the existing human approval only, and not push it into the default for every task.

If the result lands on "undetermined", write down which kind of evidence is still missing, decide under the pre-set rules whether to run another comparison, and do not keep adding until the desired answer appears.

These judgments rest on a narrow case: one parser, four briefs, and forty tool-free single-shot generations, all passing 103 checks. The case can show how to read denominators, how to distinguish three kinds of variation, and how to organize passing candidates into a regression asset; but it cannot rank any document, and it cannot vouch for a process that uses tools. The 288 slots, the 5 percentage points and the 2 minutes on the comparison card are all unexecuted design and examples, not industry standards. The self-check mapping and the P/Q archiving for each task are not yet complete either, and the CSV case is a teaching hypothesis.

Green Is Not Done went from the acceptance of one artifact to the evidence for authorizing a class of tasks. This series brings the same discipline back to the brief itself: the requirements are confirmed first by whoever has the authority to decide (the question of Part 1, Specification), and the candidates are then accepted by a yardstick that can tell known faulty versions apart and accept legitimate variation (the question of Part 2, Contract). To make an extra optional self-check the default on the grounds of improved delivery, a comparison consistent with the target work is still needed. Ten passes out of ten is the result for this one fixed task under finite checks. Once the decision reaches beyond this task, the next piece of evidence has to fill in the task scope, the yardstick's blind spots, and the human effort.

> **After all ten pass, first ask which decision the next piece of evidence is meant to change. Same-task repetition cannot by itself answer whether an optional self-check deserves cross-task adoption; the rewriting policy has to be fixed first, and then the corresponding task and human evidence obtained. Without human data, do not compute ROI; keep the status quo, or run a bounded trial under the existing human approval, and record the data the later comparison will need.**
