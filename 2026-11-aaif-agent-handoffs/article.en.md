# Before One Agent Hands Work to Another: Authority, State, and Recovery

> **TL;DR** — When one agent hands work to another, or a workspace restarts and a different agent takes over, the predecessor may have been cut off, or it may come back later. On what grounds does the next one obtain the right to take over, and decide whether to continue, redo, or stop? A handoff can carry the earlier conversation, the working files, even a claim of “approved,” but the transfer itself does not guarantee that any of it is complete, trustworthy, or still valid. What the successor has to recheck is whether the originally approved scope is still valid now, who is entitled to add operations at this moment, how far the remote system actually got, and what is still missing before the work counts as done. This article follows one hypothetical Kubernetes upgrade through six Tokyo decks, to see which kind of evidence each supplies to the successor. My judgment: a handoff must carry a task scope and a progress record that can both be checked, authorization must be rechecked at the moment of action, and completion must be confirmed against the external system. Protocols let components interoperate; those three things remain the responsibility of the whole system.

## 2. At a handoff, what exactly is handed over?

Start with a shift handover. The person going off duty leaves a note: “Machine A done, B not yet.” What the person coming on duty really needs to ask comes down to four things: whose permissions did you use to do that, whose turn is it to take over now, where exactly has the system gotten to, and what is still missing before we can close out? The note answers half of the third question. A handoff between agents is the same, except that the note has become a conversation log and a working directory.

First, separate handoff situations with two questions. The first: is control being handed over, or is work merely being farmed out while you wait for the result? When a call is transferred and the refund desk carries on with the customer while the original person withdraws, that is the former, which OpenAI's guide to building agents calls the decentralized pattern. When a manager sends someone to look something up and the result comes back to the manager, that is the latter, which the guide calls the manager pattern.[4] OpenAI's Agents SDK (a development kit) implements the former as a tool call: the new agent gets the earlier conversation by default, and the developer can filter what it sees, or have code run a check before the baton passes; the details are in the reference.[3] One subagent design described in an Anthropic engineering article leans toward the latter: a subagent receives only its own task package, never the manager's full conversation, and hands back a condensed summary.[5] The second question: does the handoff cross a service boundary, or run into a restart? Delegation across services is like an assessor handing later execution to an execution service maintained by another team; taking over after a restart means the predecessor's workspace is gone and the new agent gets only the files it left behind. The two questions combine freely. The SDK's switch is not a cross-service protocol and provides neither durable storage nor failure recovery; what this article compares is the evidence that all of these combinations need.

Put these kinds of handoff together, and what a handoff can actually pass along is the conversation and the state of the work, sometimes in condensed form. Approval records, operation results, and acceptance conditions can travel with it too, but the transfer itself does not guarantee that they are complete, trustworthy, or still valid. The conversation log may say “approved,” but that is the previous agent's claim. In Section 6 of the [Review article](https://medium.com/p/4d36d0f2f9c1) in Green Is Not Done, I wrote that a “pre-approved” claim in a PR description has to be looked up in the approval record, not read off the description; a handoff message is the same kind of input.

One concrete task runs through the rest of this article. Kubernetes is a system that coordinates how applications run across a group of machines; that group is called a cluster, and each machine a node. A platform engineer asks an agent to assess whether a particular cluster can be upgraded from 1.30 to 1.31. The initial assignment is assessment only; changing any node is not allowed. Suppose the assessment and remediation are finished and a person approves a plan; only then does an execution agent begin working through the nodes one at a time. After the request for one step has been sent, the workspace restarts. A new agent takes over the directory and finds the progress note its predecessor left: “upgrade in progress.” This is the hypothetical case that ties the sessions together; it is not an experiment run at the venue.

I place the handoff at this moment deliberately, because “request sent” cannot answer what the incoming shift cares about most. The working directory may be preserved intact while the answers remain incomplete. If the next agent receives nothing more than “finish the upgrade,” it may use its own broader permissions to supply authorization the original task never granted, or treat its predecessor's plan as something that has already happened. One more situation has to be in the picture from the start: agent A was only disconnected, agent B has already taken over, and A comes back later; both hold an approval that is still valid, so who may send the next step? Section 7 handles that question, and Section 9 tests it. The reading map for this article follows; it shows which of the six sessions give each of the four questions a basis that can be checked:

| What the shift handover must answer | What a handoff usually carries | What the successor still has to recheck | Sessions that supply the basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whose permissions, and up to what scope | A claim of “approved”; credentials | Which cluster and which plan version the approval covers, and whether it is still valid | SoftBank, Studist, Hitachi |
| Who holds the right to take over right now | A claim of “I have taken over” | The current holder's identity and handover version, and whether the predecessor has lost the right to add operations | PingCAP, and the acceptance test in Section 9 |
| How far the external system has actually gotten | Progress in the working files; the last request | The result of operations already sent; the current node state | Juicedata, PingCAP |
| What counts as done | The predecessor's plan and report | Acceptance of every target node and of the service itself | Quartic.ai |

## 3. Once a conversation is allowed, who gets to decide the next action?

In “Designing Trust Boundaries for Agent-to-Agent Systems,” Ryuji Iijima of SoftBank starts from a proof of concept and asks where identity, messages, and permitted actions should be checked when several agents work together ([official agenda](https://agntconmcpconjapan26.sched.com/event/2RCwp/designing-trust-boundaries-for-agent-to-agent-systems-lessons-learned-from-a-technical-poc-ryuji-iijima-softbank-corp), [talk recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IO6CFh_lRGw), [public slides](https://hosted-files.sched.co/agntconmcpconjapan26/3a/AGNTCon_MCPCon_Japan2026%E7%99%BB%E5%A3%87%E8%B3%87%E6%96%99_SoftBank_Corp_%E9%A3%AF%E5%B3%B6%E7%AB%9C%E5%85%90.pdf)). This session answers the first question of the handover: being able to talk to you is not the same as being able to act with your permissions.

The deck begins with an Agent Firewall. In one sentence, here is what it checks: A may contact B, but whether B may act with its own account still has to be checked separately. The firewall controls which agents may talk to each other and whether what they say gets through. The Agent-to-Agent (A2A) protocol gives different agents a common way to exchange tasks and messages, like team members handing work to one another; the Model Context Protocol (MCP) handles the interface through which an agent uses external tools and data, like each member using the company's systems. The PoC uses A2A v0.3, and page 6 limits its scope of observation to communication between agents; MCP tool execution, agents' access to data, and a complete security operations platform stay out of scope. Separating these stretches of the path is what gives the later questions a definite target.[6]

### Check communication permission separately from authority to act

His first lesson is one of definition: A2A is only one way agents interact, not what defines an agent. He defines an agent as something that “makes decisions and takes actions”; some agents never speak A2A yet can change external systems all the same. From that definition he goes on to point out: A2A tells us how agents communicate, not where trust should end. Even if A may talk to B, B may still hold its own enterprise accounts, APIs, and data-access permissions, and one permitted message can go on to trigger an action that was never inside A2A's inspection scope. Page 11 asks directly: can an action that falls outside A2A's scope be trusted? His answer is “of course not.”[6]

![Ryuji Iijima, SoftBank, slide page 12: the A2A communication boundary and a trust boundary designed around decisions, authority, and actions. Source: public conference slides; original image retained.](figures/01-softbank-boundary.png)

Back in the upgrade case, the assessment agent may ask the execution agent to “confirm whether this plan is feasible.” That request does not authorize the execution agent to drain a node. Draining means first moving whatever application workloads can be moved off that machine, so that the machine can be maintained. If the execution agent holds broader permissions and treats the text it received as full approval, “who may be contacted” has turned into “whose full power may be used.” The problem is that the downstream side supplied permissions the request never carried; confirming that both ends are genuine is not enough to decide whether to allow this action.

A handoff therefore has to let the downstream side recover at least the original task, the delegator, the target, and the scope of operations, and those conditions have to trace back to an authorization source that can be verified. If an agent can add “approved” to its own message and the next service simply complies, adding a field only swaps a self-declaration in natural language for a self-declaration in structured form. Authority is something you check, not something you read. The Review article cited in Section 2 was about PR descriptions; what this section adds is where the check sits: at the operation boundary, not where the message comes in. A model may propose the assessment result; the decision that grants execution rights must be made by a person or system entitled to make it, and checked at the operation boundary. This design requirement is my extension of the talk.

### Semantic inspection must also decide when to wait, reject, or hand over to a person

Page 13 separates two kinds of check: the connection check decides whether A may talk to B at all, and the content check decides whether this conversation should continue. Take a single request. Whether the target node is on the approved list can be settled by comparison, and that is a deterministic rule. Whether the message text smuggles in an instruction that changes the task can only be known by understanding its meaning; that is semantic inspection, where model judgment comes in. This order keeps the reproducible, lower-cost controls and places the more expensive, less stable judgment where meaning genuinely has to be understood. The design principle on pages 16 and 17 is “Rule-based by default; model-based semantic inspection only where necessary,” with one more line: “Do NOT turn every control policy intent into a prompt. Turn it into a rule first, unless meaning requires a model.”[6]

To see why semantic inspection is unstable, think of reading a letter. When a message arrives piece by piece, that is, when it is streamed, the first half of a sentence may describe a dangerous command and only the second half say “do not run it”; if each fragment is judged on its own the moment it arrives, the message is wrongly blocked. Waiting for more content adds latency. Page 15 lists the following as problems on the same control path: when to aggregate, how much context is enough, false blocks and misses, and what to do when the judgment fails. It also states plainly that “Judgment is not fully reproducible” and “Violations cannot be detected 100%.”

![SoftBank, slide page 15: enough context must accumulate before streamed content can be judged, and semantic judgment also has to handle latency, misjudgment, and failure. Source: public conference slides.](figures/02-softbank-streaming.png)

So I would not only ask “is there a safety model?” I would ask: can an action be triggered while the content is still undecided; when the judgment service times out, does the system wait, reject, or hand over to a person; and which kind of error justifies which kind of cost? Wrongly blocking an explanatory passage may cost only a wait; missing an unauthorized node drain affects the workloads. These conditions should be defined by the service owner, not left to the model to decide how much risk is acceptable.

It also matters whether the check happens before or after the effect. In the upgrade case, if what is checked is a request that has not yet reached the execution service, blocking it prevents the drain. If what is checked is the report streaming back after the operation has started, blocking the reply cannot undo a drain that has already happened; all the check can do then is detect what happened and start recovery. Waiting has a cost too: while the judgment is pending, the request keeps holding a connection and using up time. Here is how I would handle it: When semantic judgment times out, accept no new drains. A request counts as “new” until the execution service durably registers it. Keep tracking operations already registered, and do not accept requests that have passed the check but whose approval expires before they are durably registered. The cost is waiting and human intervention, and the service owner decides how much of it to bear. This is my own derivation from the case; SoftBank's PoC kept its scope of observation at the communication layer and did not validate complete operation control.

SoftBank's conclusion is that “The protocol stopped being the boundary of what we needed to govern”: the scope of control widens from the communication path to the entity that makes decisions. The next question is how to write “how far this task may go” as a limit that actually takes effect.

**After a handoff, an action that will actually change external state still has to be checked against the original task's authorization at the operation boundary; an “approved” claim in the message is only where the check begins.**

## 4. Turning this task's boundaries into limits that actually take effect

In “Intent as Code,” Masaya Nakamura of Studist presses a question: if an account has the right to operate a system, is that enough to define what the agent may do this time? He uses open-source tools to show how targets and conditions can be written as rules a program can check ([official agenda](https://agntconmcpconjapan26.sched.com/event/2QlDX/intent-as-code-why-existing-permissions-arent-enough-for-ai-masaya-nakamura-studist), [public slides](https://hosted-files.sched.co/agntconmcpconjapan26/ab/Intent-as-Code%20%2813%29.pdf)). This session answers the other half of the first question: the permissions are there, but where is the scope of this task?

His central distinction can be understood through an access badge. An engineer's badge opens every door in the company, but the engineer needs access to only one meeting room for this task. What the account can do is the larger set, and the operations the task allows are usually a small part of it. He calls the former permission (what can be done) and the latter intent (how far this task may go), and points out that the risk lies exactly where “what can be executed” exceeds “what may be executed this time.” Page 33 adds a design principle: letting the model make a non-deterministic judgment should be the last line of defense.[7]

### Shrink account capabilities to the operations this task allows

The deck lists four risk examples. I go through the third in full, because it can change the automated process that runs afterward. First, the everyday meaning of a few words: after changing code, an engineer saves a version locally (a commit) and then sends it to the remote code repository (a push to the repo); the remote then automatically runs builds and tests, a process called continuous integration (CI), whose definition is itself a file kept in the repository. An agent that can push can therefore change the CI configuration in passing and have CI act on its behalf with the permissions available through CI's own account and secrets. These secrets are the passwords or API keys CI needs to log into other systems. While fixing code, the agent changes the process that checks its own work. The other three examples (mistaken edits to issues or leaks of internal information, external code loaded during a Terraform preview, and instructions hidden in a web page) are in the notes at the end.

His countermeasures are a handful of command-line interface (CLI) wrapper tools: each wraps a layer of checking around the original tool, and every command passes through that check before it is sent. Everything below is an example configuration from the slides. `safe-gh` restricts reads to allowlisted organizations; in the demo, the issue-editing rule requires an issue in the designated organization, authored by oneself and carrying an AI annotation. Creating issues, commenting, and other operations are not shown. With a shared account, “authored by oneself” is not the same as “created by this agent,” and an issue the AI created may have nothing to do with the current task. So these rules first shrink the set of possible operations; restricting them to “this task” still needs a separate check that the issue belongs to the task. `safe-push` inspects the committed files before a push, refuses or asks for human approval when they touch CI definitions such as `.github/`, keeps a human `--force` path, and restricts the remote target. A path check protects the listed scope, but a script that CI loads from some other path can still change CI's behavior, so a complete check has to follow the dependency path, at the cost of maintenance and false blocks. `safe-webfetch` handles reading web pages; see the notes. Their shared direction is to define the target, the operation, and the conditions first, then have a program check them, so that a request outside those conditions is blocked before it actually executes.[7]

Back in the upgrade case, the assessment agent may read cluster versions and resources, but that does not grant it permission to drain, modify, or delete nodes. Once the work reaches the stage where the cluster has to change, the execution service should check the target, steps, and conditions approved for this task. This guards against more than malicious content: even an agent trying in good faith to finish the job can mistake “this change is faster” for “I may do this while I am at it.”

### Put the enforcing check where the agent cannot route around it

Where a limit sits, though, decides how much responsibility it can carry. The wrapper is the layer just described; a hook inserts a check at a specified moment. Page 58 points straight at the limit of such local controls: if the agent can still call the original CLI or API, obtain the same credentials, or even rewrite the restriction settings, the extra wrapped check can be bypassed. His conclusion is to enforce the boundary outside the environment the agent can operate on: page 59 moves the operations that need permissions into a managed remote execution environment, which verifies the target and conditions, uses the necessary permissions, and returns the result. Both the credentials and the judgment of “may this run” are separated out of the agent's workspace.[7]

![Masaya Nakamura, Studist, slide page 58: local wrappers and hooks can still leave bypass paths; enforcement has to sit in an environment the agent cannot modify. Source: public conference slides; original image in Japanese.](figures/03-intent-enforcement.png)

This changed how I would accept a claim that “a hook has been added.” The check should follow every path that can reach the target: after the restricted tool refuses a request, can the direct API still complete the same operation? Can the agent change the control file and call again? The key is not where the machine is but three things: can the agent modify the policy, can it obtain the underlying credentials, and can it bypass the checking entry point? The same machine can hold controls the agent has no right to modify, and a remote service can also accept requests that are overly permissive.

Sections 3 and 6 of [The Harness Blueprint](https://medium.com/p/3facc281f633), Part 2 of my Agentic Engineering series, already required secrets held on the tool side, a short-lived, narrowly scoped credential for each run (one execution of the agent, from launch to finish), outbound connections denied by default, and paths that cannot be bypassed. The agent holds only a reference to the key; when the reference arrives, the tool side still verifies who may use it, and holding the reference does not mean the key can be obtained. What this article adds is the part that crosses runs: once the same task has moved to a new run, the identifiers of existing operations must stay the same, while the identity of the one taking over changes and the old run loses the right to add operations. Walking the path once makes this clear. The agent hands over a request to “process node N”; the entry point that receives requests (the gateway) checks the task and the target; the tool runs in an isolated environment that opens only the necessary directories and connections (the sandbox); and the key it needs is obtained at the moment of execution by a service acting on its behalf (the secret broker). From start to finish, the agent touches neither the rules it has to obey nor the key.

For this upgrade, one design worth discussing is an execution service that accepts a limited request of the form “process this node under this version of the plan,” checks the plan against the current conditions, and only then acts with its own restricted credentials. The agent never needs a credential that can change the whole cluster at will. Where the task rules come from, and how they map to an approved version, also have to be settled: the plan version and the permitted steps are written into the execution service by the approval flow, and the agent's request can only reference them, never carry its own. Moving the policy to a remote service only changes who can modify it; it does not automatically make the policy correct. The cost is that the execution service must spell out which steps it supports and how it reports errors, and someone has to maintain it as operations are added. The more general the operation interface, the more likely it is to re-expose arbitrary execution; the more specialized it is, the more maintenance and exception handling it needs. I would limit the service to operations that recur within the task and whose effect can be described, and leave the rest to people; what the design buys in return is a boundary that can be checked directly. This is my design suggestion, not a Kubernetes implementation the deck delivered.

### Use permitted and out-of-bounds requests to check that the limit takes effect

To verify, first send one request that matches the approval, then keep every other condition the same and change only the target to an unapproved node, or only the step to an operation not in the plan. The request that matches the approval should proceed; both out-of-bounds requests should be rejected before any external effect occurs. Only then do you know that the limit is based on the task scope, not merely on recognizing this account. A local wrapper can still flag common mistakes early and keep developers off the failure path, but when the same executor can rewrite it, the final enforcement has to be carried by another boundary.

These limits give the work an enforceable boundary, but they leave another problem: even when no operation exceeds its authority, the system may act on a stale assessment and do the wrong thing in the right format. The Kubernetes session puts “may execute” and “suitable to execute now” into the same workflow.

**Whether a task limit is effective depends on whether every path that can reach an external operation makes its allow-or-reject decision from the same authorized scope.**

## 5. Recommending, approving, and accepting an upgrade need different evidence

In “Letting an Agent Upgrade Production Kubernetes,” Sanskar Agrawalla and Abhijeet Chaudhuri of Quartic.ai put an agent inside a cluster upgrade workflow and discuss, in order, collecting data, assessing compatibility, obtaining approval, executing, and verifying ([official agenda](https://agntconmcpconjapan26.sched.com/event/2QlD9/letting-an-agent-upgrade-production-kubernetes-without-getting-paged-at-3-am-sanskar-agrawalla-abhijeet-chaudhuri-quarticai), [public slides](https://hosted-files.sched.co/agntconmcpconjapan26/d9/AGNTCon-MCPCon-Japan-2026_Abhijeet_Sanskar_final.pdf)). This session answers the fourth question of the handover, what counts as done, and the answer was learned from a failure.

First, the whole picture: in this upgrade workflow, no step's conclusion can be taken directly as evidence for the next step. The next paragraph sets out the demo environment and the five execution conditions the speakers list. “An upgrade judged feasible still needs approval to execute” follows data collection, rule checks, and model assessment to show how the four assessment states are kept apart from human approval; along the way, it uses a single request that creates a workload to explain the API server, admission webhooks, and controllers. “After remediation, reconfirm the assessment and the approval” covers which cluster and which plan version an approval has to be bound to, and which changes in the environment require a new approval. “Accept layer by layer, from every node to the service outcome” goes from the failure of checking only the first node to tool versions, the verifier, and service acceptance, and then to the four things that have to be handled separately when an approval expires. The extra evidence a handoff has to carry is concentrated in the last two subsections.

Their demonstration environment is a local test cluster built with kind, with the model served through a local Ollama instance; page 17 states this clearly. Of the two, kind is a tool for building a test cluster on a local machine, and Ollama is a service for running models locally; the deck does not say which model version was used.[8] The upgrade itself is a single command. The hard part is everything that has to be confirmed before that command: removed APIs, the compatibility of the various add-ons and components, whether the backup can be restored, and the conditions for rollback. Their question is direct: what has to hold before an agent may execute? Page 11 gives five answers: evidence over opinion, machine-readable decisions, a hard stop on a not-recommended result, human approval for irreversible actions, and independent verification of completion.

### An upgrade judged feasible still needs approval to execute

What makes this case most worth reading is that it does not compress the upgrade into one big prompt. In the flow on page 14, a data collector first gathers cluster data, rules check it, and a model assesses it and produces a report. The checkpoint that decides whether the flow may continue is called a gate; only past it does the work move on to remediation and execution, with a verification component confirming the result at the end. Each step answers a different question and also limits what the previous step's answer can stand for. Page 13 divides the labor this way: the agent decides what to do next; the system decides what it is allowed to do. Why is a model needed at all? Rules can check known conditions; compatibility notes, change logs, and vendor announcements are unstructured text, and judging “does this document cover our configuration” is where a model earns its place. When no compatibility evidence can be found, the model may not make up an answer; it can only report the gap, which is why the UNKNOWN state below exists. Compared with a fully fixed automated flow, what the model saves is the time people spend reading documents, and the price is that its judgment has to be framed by the assessment states and the approval.

The data collector can only observe. Page 19 puts `get`, `version`, `top`, `api-resources`, and `cluster-info` on a read-only list and rejects mutating operations at the function boundary. Next come pure Python rules, checking the known conditions that make upgrades fail: an old deployment format that the new version no longer accepts (removed APIs), an add-on version that does not yet support the target version (a vendor support matrix encoded as rules), incompatible controllers, webhooks that would block the process, and version gaps. To see what the terms webhook and controller mean, follow one request that creates a workload. The request first reaches the cluster's front desk (the API server). If it matches a configured rule, the front desk first calls an external checking service; that call point is an admission webhook. Once the request is let through, the program that continually adjusts the actual state toward what the configuration asks for (the controller) takes over. If the request must pass that checking service, the service does not support the new version, and it is configured to reject on failure, then after the upgrade this kind of new workload cannot be created. A controller on an incompatible version, for its part, may stop adjusting. Before the upgrade, it is necessary to confirm that these interdependent components can still work together. The rules produce counts, not prose, and “Unverified compatibility is treated as risk, not as silence.”

Page 21 parses the assessment result into four states: `APPROVED` still needs human approval; `CONDITIONAL` requires remediation first or human acceptance of an explicit, bounded risk; `NOT RECOMMENDED` blocks execution; and when the data or output cannot be reliably interpreted, the state is `UNKNOWN`, which likewise blocks execution. The slide's title is “The Model Can Recommend. It Cannot Authorize.” At the end there is one more hard stop: the agent pauses and asks “Proceed with the upgrade? [y/n]” and requires an explicit answer; answering y starts the upgrade, and answering n stops it.

![Sanskar Agrawalla and Abhijeet Chaudhuri, Quartic.ai, slide page 21: assessment state is kept separate from human approval, and UNKNOWN cannot be treated as a pass. Source: public conference slides.](figures/04-upgrade-gate.png)

Two places in the figure matter most: “assessment passed” is still followed by a decision, and “cannot interpret” has an exit of its own. The three gates in Section 6 of the Green Is Not Done overview check test discrimination, review, and task reliability, and the Testing article already required that an unexpected failure not count as a pass. This session is another application of the same principle, turning upgrade readiness into four machine-readable states. What this article really adds is the question that follows: after a handoff, how do the assessment, the approval, and the current state get realigned?

### After remediation, reconfirm the assessment and the approval

Why reassess after remediation? Because the original report describes the cluster before the repair. Replacing one deprecated API may at the same time change how a controller is deployed; the other conditions that held before do not automatically keep holding because that one item turned green. Page 22 requires going back to assessment after remediation: “No bypass. No ‘just continue.’”

For example, incomplete compatibility data for a webhook and a confirmed finding that the webhook does not support the target version are two different reasons to block. The first calls for finding the maintainer or obtaining verifiable data; the second calls for scheduling a fix and then observing again. If both are collapsed into a single low-confidence score, the person taking over can hardly tell whether to gather data or change the system. The value of keeping the assessment state is that the next piece of work has a direction, and human approval can then be aimed at a concrete plan and the remaining risk.

I would extend this design one step further: the approval record should map to a specific cluster, a specific plan version, and a specific observation of the environment. This is my design suggestion; the slides do not show that complete approval binding and expiry mechanisms have been implemented.

The environment keeps changing after approval, but not every change should call for a new signature, or the flow would stall; carrying the approval forward regardless, though, means executing a stale plan. I would sort changes into three kinds. The first kind is change that does not affect the approval and needs only to be recorded: a state transition the plan expects, such as a node's version reaching 1.31 in sequence, or an observation update that matches the earlier one, such as a new health check result. The second kind is variation within the pre-approved bounds, such as a target node's resource usage fluctuating within the range the approval stated, where the assessment is rerun and the approved targets and steps carry over. The third kind is variation beyond the bounds, such as a change to the node list (an added node, for instance) or to the roles, a swapped controller, a change in controller or webhook versions, a different plan regenerated by the agent, or a changed target version; in that case the approval lapses and fresh approval is required.

The contract I adopt here is that the approval binds to a fixed list of node identities, and an added node means revising the scope. The alternative is to approve a dynamic set defined by explicit conditions, which then has to be bounded separately by role, count, capacity, and maintenance limits, with any added node having to prove it meets them. Either works; what does not is demanding that approval bind to targets while letting “same role” widen them by default. So what the approval has to state is not just a snapshot but the conditions of acceptable variation, and before the next operation the execution service checks those conditions rather than relying on the agent's own claims. When acceptance is declared, there is one more inconsistency to handle: if the node list at approval time does not match the current node list, completion cannot be declared. Nor is it acceptable to leave behind a bare `approved: true` for every later version to inherit. This is exactly what the first row of the table in Section 2 says the successor still has to recheck: “Which cluster and which plan version the approval covers.”

### Accept layer by layer, from every node to the service outcome

Once the approved list is fixed, who confirms that the executor did not miss one of the nodes? Page 29 candidly records a failure: the first version of the logic looked only at the first node and treated the whole cluster as upgraded. A cluster's nodes have two roles: control plane nodes coordinate the cluster, and workers carry the application workloads. In a case with three control plane nodes and three workers, the control plane had reached 1.31 while the workers were still on 1.30, yet that hop was marked a success. After the fix, the executor finds every node by role and processes them in order, and the next version step does not start until every node is in place. Their conclusion: “A cluster upgrade succeeds only when the whole cluster has crossed the version boundary.”[8]

![Quartic.ai, slide page 29: the first version checked only the first node; the fix identifies node roles, upgrades node by node, and confirms every node before the next version step. Source: public conference slides.](figures/05-upgrade-workers.png)

I borrowed this failure in Section 5 of the [Testing article](https://medium.com/p/51d001a6dcd5) in Green Is Not Done, where the point was that the checker itself has to be checked: a check that samples only part of the boundary reports green. Placed in a handoff, the failure carries one more layer of meaning. If the executor misses the workers, and the verifier checks only the nodes the executor reports as “the ones I processed,” the two miss the same set of targets together. The source of the acceptance list therefore matters: my verification design would build its expectations from the cluster inventory and target requirements at the time of approval, then independently query each node's identity, role, and version. A test can deliberately leave one target worker on the old version while every other node meets the goal; if the check still declares completion, it is verifying the successes it happened to see. After the fix, keep one normal case where everything meets the goal too, to confirm that the check does not become so conservative that it can never finish. The Testing article said the checker has to be checked; here I take it one step further: the acceptance list may not inherit the executor's omissions.

The tools themselves can change the outcome too. Page 30 records that an intended upgrade from 1.30 to 1.31 pulled in version 1.32 of kubeadm, the upgrade tool, because the package was not pinned. The 1.32 tool refused to upgrade a 1.30 control plane, and the upgrade stopped before it began. The fix was to pin the tool version for each hop and clear out stale package sources. Their lesson: the cluster version is not the only version that has to be controlled; the upgrade tool, too, has to move one minor version at a time.[8] Kubernetes' own rules likewise require one minor version per hop.[9] Once the successor knows which nodes remain, it still has to ask whether the tools in hand can continue the original plan. This adds one more thing to the handover: which tool version the predecessor used, and from which package source; without the same tool version, the original plan cannot be continued.

Finally, infrastructure checks and service acceptance have to be kept apart. The verifier on page 27 asks five questions: are all nodes Ready, are they all on the target version, is the API service available, are any Pods stuck in an abnormal state, and are the admission webhooks still there? A Pod is the unit in which Kubernetes runs an application; a node showing Ready means it reports being ready to take on work. These five questions can support the claim that “the cluster meets these technical conditions,” but whether a given business transaction still succeeds needs the service's own verification. The slide puts it this way: “A Successful Command Does Not Mean a Healthy Cluster.” I would add one more line: cluster health is not service acceptance either. The platform team should hand the cluster evidence to the service owner, so that the two sides decide together whether maintenance is over. Approval expiry also has to be split into four things: when to stop accepting new operations; whether operations already accepted run on to completion; when results may be confirmed; and who handles an overrun of the maintenance window. Expiry closes only the first. It must not erase the effects of operations that were legitimately accepted before expiry and confirmed complete only afterward; whether the work must finish inside the window is a separate contract to be stated explicitly. All four need to be tested separately.

At this point, approval, assessment, and acceptance each have their own evidence. But once the workspace is gone, which pieces of that evidence can still be found? This is the very moment at which Section 2 deliberately placed the handoff: after one step's request has been sent, the predecessor's environment is gone, and the agent taking over gets only the directory, with the “upgrade in progress” note inside it. The next session answers that question from the storage layer.

**Whether an upgrade can end is decided jointly by an approval scope that matches the current environment, completion evidence for every target, and the service's own acceptance.**

## 6. After the workspace restarts, which state should be restored?

In “Stateful Sandboxes for Stateless Agents,” Rui Su of Juicedata takes up an easily overlooked problem: an agent's execution environment can be short-lived, while its working files and results may need to be kept for the next run ([official agenda](https://agntconmcpconjapan26.sched.com/event/2QlE7/stateful-sandboxes-for-stateless-agents-designing-durable-volumes-for-mcp-tool-workspaces-rui-su-juicedata), [public slides](https://hosted-files.sched.co/agntconmcpconjapan26/4e/Stateful%20Sandboxes%20for%20Stateless%20Agents%20%C2%B7%20designed-r3.pdf)). This session answers the third question of the handover: once the environment is gone, where does the successor learn how far the work got?

The sandbox this session uses is an isolated working environment that can be discarded with the task, like a workroom rented by the hour: in it, the agent checks out code, edits it, installs dependencies, runs tests, and writes a report, and when the agent checks out, the whole room is cleared. Isolation and restriction are what make it a sandbox; being disposable is this session's lifecycle choice. Page 2 uses those five tool calls to make the point that “The agent is stateless. The work is not.” The five calls accumulate a unique state on the same filesystem; when the environment is gone after the tests, which things must stay, and which should disappear? MCP specifies the format of a tool call, not the workspace behind the call: which directories are mounted, which are shared, which persist, and how to recover after a failure are still for the platform to decide.[10]

### First separate the data to keep from the permissions to recheck

The most intuitive approach is to save the whole directory. But page 4 warns that “Mechanism is not intent”: in the deck's words, “A volume can retain a secret too long and still lose an edit at the wrong commit boundary.” A directory holds original inputs, temporary files, finished artifacts, operation progress, and credentials all at once; sitting on neighboring paths does not mean they should share one lifecycle. Page 5 splits state into six classes, turning “keep it or not” into a set of choices that can be discussed.

![Rui Su, Juicedata, slide page 5: inputs, working files, cache, artifacts, control state, and secrets each need their own way of being kept and recovered. Source: public conference slides.](figures/06-state-classes.png)

Read-only, versioned inputs tell you where this round of work started; mutable working files let it resume after an interruption and let someone inspect the changes; cache can usually be regenerated even if it is lost; artifacts need explicit identification and a publishing step so that a half-finished product is not mistaken for a delivered one. The other two classes matter more. Control state covers who approved what and which operation started or ended, and the deck argues it belongs in an external, ordered, auditable record; secrets such as access credentials should be obtained at run time and never put into any snapshot.

Putting control state outside and keeping secrets out of snapshots is a storage arrangement; it does not turn into a re-authorization flow by itself. Three more things have to go with it; these are my suggestions: the approval record may be written only by a person or system with the authority; on taking over, the successor queries the authorization service for the conditions valid now; and if nothing can be found, it pauses pending confirmation rather than carrying on with anything from an old snapshot. An external record can also be stale, or even rewritten by the agent, so “kept outside” is not a synonym for “trusted.” Only with these in place does restoring the working directory recover just the material the analysis needs, leaving the question of whether the permissions are still valid to the authorization service at that moment. This is also the design suggestion this section puts forward: credentials should not travel along with the working directory. The next session's deck shows one usable capability: different customers each have their own data scope (a tenant), and an agent can be issued a pass valid for one hour that allows reads and writes only under `/repo/docs`.[11] I would use it to design the re-credentialing step at remount, so that what the successor gets is short-lived, restricted access rather than the predecessor's credentials. The deck does not show re-credentialing integrated with the transfer of the right to take over; that part is my suggestion.

### Local progress and remote results can come apart at an interruption

Back in the upgrade flow, when the next agent reads a file saying “node A processed,” all it has is a statement left by the previous process. It still needs to compare that statement against the external operation record and then query the cluster's current state. If the request before the restart was sent but its result was never written back locally, resending may cause a duplicate action; if the local file recorded completion first while the remote side had not yet succeeded, skipping leaves work undone. Storage can keep the file, but the relationship between those two moments has to be handled by the operation workflow.

Reordering the file writes alone cannot close this gap, because the local write and the remote change each have their own moment of completion, and an interruption can still fall between them. Recovery needs to query the operations already sent and the target's state, then write what it finds back into the progress record. This is the application's recovery responsibility, and “the directory has a backup” cannot stand in for it.

### Define the durability promise first, then choose where to inject failures

Page 7 asks for durability to be written as a contract across the boundary. Think of saving a game: which action counts as a real save? How much progress is it acceptable to lose? How long until you are back at a point where you can keep playing? The deck's version is three promises, each asked about separately. First, when does it count as saved (the commit point)? After which action (a write, a file close, or an `fsync` that forces data to disk) is an edit that has been acknowledged as “saved” truly safe from loss? Second, how much may be lost (the recovery point objective, or RPO)? That is, how much acknowledged work may be lost. Here I would split the promise again: an analysis draft can be rebuilt, while an operation identifier that has been sent must not be lost, so the two should not share one promise. In practice, that means plan drafts and analysis notes may stay local, operation identifiers and approval references must be durably registered before a request is sent, and finished reports have to be published to the artifact area. When the durability layer is unavailable, read-only analysis may continue while every operation that cannot be safely resent is stopped; that is a design cost. Third, how fast is recovery (the recovery time objective, or RTO)? That is, how quickly another sandbox can remount and read that edit. None of the three means the remote operation has completed. After that come retention periods and who is allowed to restore, fork, publish, or delete. Seeing an `fsync` call does not by itself let you assume every remote replica has completed the write.[10]

This also changes the tests I would choose. Page 13 says that “If you only benchmark throughput, you have not tested durability” and lists the failures to inject: kill the process to check the commit point and lost writes; kill the sandbox to check restart and remount; take down a node to see how long recovery from elsewhere takes; cut the network to check errors, retries, and concurrent writes; and confirm that secrets never enter a snapshot. I would add the cut points specific to the upgrade case: interrupt the process before the request is sent, after the remote side has completed but before the local record is written, and before the working files have been confirmed durable; then check whether recovery establishes the current state, whether it executes anything twice, and whether it loses data that was promised to be kept. This test design extends the deck's direction; this article does not claim to have run it.

When work is handed to two agents at the same time, one more problem has to be handled: when both believe they hold the latest version, whose change gets to stay? That is the part the next session pursues further, starting from concurrent work.

**When recovering work, keep the material needed to continue, and recheck both the results of external operations and the current authorization before deciding the next step.**

## 7. No file was overwritten, and the work can still conflict

In “Externalizing Agent State,” Tadatoshi Sekiguchi of PingCAP introduces two kinds of content that need to be kept across execution environments: the memory that lets an agent recall past work, and the files it actually produces. He uses the open-source mem9 and drive9 to show how memory, file storage, and multiple workspaces fit together ([official agenda](https://agntconmcpconjapan26.sched.com/event/2QlEe/externalizing-agent-state-memory-and-filesystem-management-for-sandboxed-coding-agents-tadatoshi-sekiguchi-pingcap), [public slides](https://hosted-files.sched.co/agntconmcpconjapan26/17/EN%20_GNTCon-Japan-2026_PingCAP%20Seki.pdf)). This session pushes the third question of the handover to the case where two people take over at the same time, and it also answers the second: who holds the right to take over.

His starting point is the same as the previous session's: split the agent into the control program that reads the task, calls tools, and moves on to the next step (what he calls the stateless harness, which does not need local user data to be restored after a rebuild) and the user's data that has to be kept separately, and put the data in a dedicated persistence layer, like the database behind a traditional application. The second of the two projects, drive9, provides a cloud workspace through a filesystem interface, so an agent can work with ordinary Unix tools and git; files are written to a local cache first and then uploaded to the remote asynchronously.[11]

### A version check preserves the edit and the opportunity to handle the conflict

Asynchronous upload means that what the agent can see locally is not necessarily what the remote has received; a recovery guarantee has to say which completion signal it waits for. Section 6 already borrowed the tenant and the short-lived task pass; here we look only at the files themselves.

The conflict handled on page 23 is much like two people editing the same shared document at once. A client reads a version number when it opens the file and carries that version when it writes back; if the version on the server has moved on, the write is refused, and the local edit remains with the client whose write failed. This is called optimistic locking: allow concurrent work first, and only at commit time check whether this edit still rests on the same starting point.

![Tadatoshi Sekiguchi, PingCAP, English deck page 23: a write carries the version read earlier, the overwrite is refused when the remote version has changed, and the local edit that could not be written back is kept. Source: public conference slides.](figures/07-file-conflict.png)

In terms of the upgrade plan: A and B both start from version 7; A fixes webhook compatibility and saves version 8, while B, still working from version 7, adds the worker steps. Accepting B's whole file would make A's change disappear. Refusing B's stale write preserves the opportunity to recognize the conflict and, for the moment, keeps B's local edit. Yet a sandbox can disappear and uploads are asynchronous, so after the refusal the conflicting copy first has to be saved as a durably queryable artifact, and the environment cleaned up only once that save is confirmed; otherwise B's edit can still be lost. Next, B still has to check the worker steps together with the new webhook conditions. The version number knows only that the starting point has changed; it cannot judge whether the two plans can hold together. Automatically rereading and then overwriting with the same content could wipe out the very change that was just protected. Conflict handling should keep both sides' reasons and differences before deciding to merge, redo, or hand the decision to a person.

Parallel exploration is worth that cost of merging and revalidating only when the branches really need to change the same thing at once; work that only reads needs no branch. Shared edits conflict easily; can exploration be isolated first? The deck answers with “layers” built with copy-on-write: two branches start from the same initial files, are modified on their own without affecting each other, and are finally committed back to the mainline or discarded, which suits an agent's exploration and trial and error. What layers isolate is files: external operations, credentials, and network effects are not isolated by them.

### Each file can be correct and the combination still violate the task requirement

Different files can also have no version conflict and still have a work conflict. Suppose the plan requires that only one worker be processed at a time. A and B each edit their own working file and each decide to start on a different worker. Every file is written from its latest version, yet together they violate the limit on concurrent operations. This example is my extension of the analysis: a file version protects an update to one piece of content, while the task requirement cares about the effect of two actions taken together.

Here the execution service can issue maintenance slots: a step with side effects obtains a slot before it starts and releases it only once the operation is confirmed finished, while read-only assessment needs no slot and keeps running in parallel. That avoids locking the whole long-running analysis just to prevent operation conflicts. The scope of a slot has to be stated: one slot per node, one for the whole cluster, or slots issued against shared capacity? A single slot for the whole cluster is simplest but may slow things down needlessly; too narrow a scope fails to protect the task condition. The requirement in this case is that only one worker is processed at a time, so there is exactly one slot, issued and reclaimed by the execution service rather than each side reading a number of its own. If the coordinator that designates the successor (not the execution service) itself fails, operations already accepted keep being tracked and new ones are held pending confirmation.

Running operations one at a time through the slot still leaves another question unanswered: A was only disconnected and comes back later, B has already taken over, and both hold a task approval that is still valid, so who may send the next step? The execution service must know who the current holder is, and someone must have the authority to designate them. The approach is to treat the takeover as a record updated by a trusted coordinator or an authorized person: the task, the current holder's execution identity (the identity of this run), the handover version, and the scope of operations. All four are updated together, and that update and the acceptance of requests must be consistently ordered. The handover version is not a number that anyone who reads it can use; it is bound to the holder's identity. A request must carry both the version and the identity, and the execution service accepts it only if both match the current record. So when A comes back, even with an unexpired credential, it is refused because its identity is no longer the holder's. If A reads the latest version and sends a request with it, the request still fails the identity check. Three races have to be tested: an old request from A arriving late, A impersonating the holder with the latest version, and B and C asking to take over at the same time. Checking a name or an account cannot handle these. The results of operations already accepted keep being tracked. Checking authorization and preventing two holders from acting at once are two different requirements; without the latter, a handoff has only passed data along and has not transferred responsibility for execution. This is my extension of the analysis. A request already sent whose result is unknown still needs an operation identifier and a result query; the fact that the file conflict has been resolved does not let you assume the external work has been picked up and completed as well.

### Memory helps recover the context; the approval still has to be looked up

Memory in mem9 is a freshness problem of a different kind: a memory summarized as “the engineer agreed to the upgrade” can only help locate the source of the approval at the time. It cannot replace an approval record that is still valid; Part 3 takes this up.[11]

At this point, the checks on authorization, the right to take over, state, and completion are scattered across different components. Each component has its own security features, yet the business flow they form together can still drop a condition; the next session starts from exactly such an observation: “ordered even though a condition was not met.”

**File versions protect edits and memory helps recover context, but whether an action may continue still has to be judged from the task scope and the current state.**

## 8. The components are all connected; who is responsible for the whole workflow?

In “What's Missing in the Open Agentic Stack,” Tatsuya Sato and Satoshi Ito of Hitachi use a procurement workflow to compare ways of integrating different open-source components, discussing how agent communication, work dispatch, authentication, and observability connect, and who still maintains them once connected ([official agenda](https://agntconmcpconjapan26.sched.com/event/2QlEG/whats-missing-in-the-open-agentic-stack-lessons-from-oss-integration-tatsuya-sato-satoshi-ito-hitachi-ltd), [public slides](https://hosted-files.sched.co/agntconmcpconjapan26/bf/20260910_AGNTCon_Japan_Hitachi_Ito_Sato_public.pdf)). This session puts the four questions the earlier sessions took apart back onto a single work path.

The procurement case has clear steps: requirement, budget, compliance, approval, and ordering each have their own agent and system, and the expected rule is that “the order is placed only after every condition and the human approval are complete.” But on page 24 they record that, in the demo, an order was placed in some cases although a condition or approval had not yet been satisfied. In their terms, one procurement assignment is a task, each launch of an agent to work on it is a run, and a price lookup or an order is a tool call within it. The deck's technology survey is dated August 20, 2026, and should be read as a comparison of reference implementations at that time, not as a product ranking that stays valid.[12]

### Three ways to integrate: where does each put the maintenance responsibility?

The first approach wires A2A and MCP together directly: agent addresses are kept by the application as a static list, agents connect directly to each other and to tools, and Keycloak's token exchange passes the user's permissions downstream, much as a purchasing role presents its own ID to exchange it for a pass the target system accepts (Keycloak handles identity and access management). Whether the pass's permissions have actually been narrowed has to be configured and verified separately, and holding the pass does not mean this order has been approved. This choice gives the team control over more of the details, and also makes the team responsible for connecting them.

The second approach uses AGNTCY's shared facilities, which between them answer four questions: whom to find, what it can do, how to pass the message, and what it has done (the component names are in the notes), so each team writes a little less integration code of its own. The deck cautions that proof of an agent's identity is not the user's delegation: knowing who is taking over, and knowing how much money it may spend on the user's behalf this time, are two questions that need different evidence.

The third approach uses agentgateway to centralize connection, authentication, and observability at a shared entry point. According to page 20, the gateway rewrites and distributes agent addresses, so callers need no code changes; traces, logs, and metrics for traffic that passes through the gateway can be collected without changing the agents; and the gateway validates the origin and intended audience of access credentials at the entry point and controls access to agents and MCP servers on that basis. An entry point that can validate the credential still does not know what this task was approved to do. The cost is spelled out on page 23: there is no control outside the gateway, and how an agent advances the work internally cannot be seen.

In Section 3 of [The Harness Blueprint](https://medium.com/p/3facc281f633), I suggested that a first MCP gateway start from a tool inventory (a registry), authorization checks, a place to obtain restricted credentials (an identity broker), and an operation record (an audit log). Hitachi's third approach is close to that design in where the central entry point sits, but end-to-end user delegation remains a gap, and page 20 lists it explicitly as something you have to build yourself. The missing piece of data is “what this task was approved to do,” and the component that should obtain and enforce it is the one that executes the operation, not the entry point that merely validates credentials. Treating the three approaches as input to a selection decision, I would first look at which part of the maintenance the team is prepared to take on. With few counterparts and fixed endpoints, direct integration keeps the necessary paths clear, but the connection and observability code has to be maintained in-house; when agents are frequently added or replaced, discovery and description mechanisms answer the problem directly; and only when several teams need to share access policies does a central entry point justify its maintenance. The selection table also has to answer three questions directly: which component reads the approval, which component refuses the order, and which team maintains the conditions. How convenient the connectivity is cannot answer any of the three. A shared entry point also concentrates the impact of outages and policy errors: when the entry point is unavailable, read-only queries can wait while change operations must stop, and the two deserve different handling. These are selection conditions I derive from the comparison; the architecture names in the deck cannot simply be ranked from better to worse.

### Shared components still need the business-condition checks wired in

What all three leave behind is responsibility for the business conditions. Page 24 lists three gaps. The first is that end-to-end observability is heavy: the gateway records “who accessed what” and OpenTelemetry (a standard for recording how programs execute) records “why,” which is the slide's summary. To rebuild the timeline of one procurement, from assignment through the agent's processing to the ordering service, every segment has to be instrumented in advance and the records correlated. Even then, a timeline proves only the order of events, not an approval. The slide's conclusion is that the gateway's records and OpenTelemetry's each leave an audit gap when used alone. The second is that there is no consistent approach to end-to-end delegation and authorization: when the Agent → Agent → Tool → Resource path is decided dynamically, teams still have to build their own mechanisms for deciding whose permissions to use, what to delegate downstream, and how to narrow permissions at each hop. The third is the observation from the opening: an order placed although a condition or approval had not yet been satisfied.[12]

![Tatsuya Sato and Satoshi Ito, Hitachi, slide page 24: the A2A task, agent run, and tool call need to be linked, permissions narrowed step by step, and the procurement flow verified to actually respect its conditions and approvals. Source: public conference slides.](figures/08-integration-gaps.png)

The deck gives no count or denominator from which a failure rate could be calculated, and I will not inflate this observation into a claim that every related implementation will fail. But the example is enough to overturn the inference that “every component has security features, so the whole workflow must respect the approval.” A procurement approval may limit the amount, the items, and the deadline; an upgrade approval may limit the cluster and the steps. If the connecting component never obtains and checks that information, it has no way of knowing whether the well-formed request in front of it exceeds the task. Selection therefore has to include “who implements this check, and where does its data come from?” Going one layer deeper: if the conditions for ordering are already known, why let a model decide whether an order may be placed at all? Put the conditions into the ordering service's deterministic state transitions, so that the workflow cannot reach an orderable state until all conditions are met, and let the agent handle data collection and exceptions. The price is that someone has to maintain those business rules.

### Link the task, run, and tool call back to the same authorization

One assignment may pass through retries and handoffs, so the links among the three levels (task, run, and tool call) need to be kept. I would trace back from that order: which task triggered which run, which run issued which tool call, and which approval was it relying on at the time? These identifiers have to be related to one another before anyone can find the step at which the requirement was dropped. Complete records, though, only make the investigation afterward easier. To prevent a recurrence, the boundary that executes the order has to check the necessary conditions before sending and stop the action when the check fails. Being able to trace a record to a person still does not mean someone has taken ownership of the case. In Section 2 of the [organization piece](https://medium.com/p/92343384d987) in my Agentic Engineering series, the responsibility matrix (RACI) gives the shared environment and permissions to the platform, and gives the domain's MCP tools, product quality, and responsibility for quality and incidents after release (production ownership) to the team that owns the product (the domain team). Applied to a handoff, the platform maintains the shared mechanisms for operation identifiers and execution coordination; the domain team owns the semantics of each operation, its state queries, and its remediation contract; and the service owner defines business acceptance and the risk that is acceptable. A result that remains unknown after a timeout is picked up by the on-call engineer, but the service owner makes the final call on recovering, stopping, or proposing a separate remedy.

What holds for procurement holds for the upgrade. The trust boundary of Section 3, the task limits of Section 4, and the approval binding of Section 5 join here into a single chain, and the handover version from Section 7 adds the last link: each tool call maps back not only to an approval but also to the current handover version, so a late request from the predecessor finds nothing to attach itself to.

**Once the components are connected, the system still has to check the task and the approval before every external action, and leave a record that can be traced back to the person responsible.**

## 9. If I were doing the acceptance now, I would start from one interrupted handoff

Once Section 8 has joined up the chain of responsibility, one interrupted handoff can test the whole chain. The acceptance question I find most useful is this: **with the predecessor absent, or with the predecessor back again, can the next one still make a well-grounded decision?** I would start in a disposable test environment and run acceptance tests on only one kind of limited operation. What follows is a test design drawn from these decks, with no results from an actual run. Nor is it an upgrade manual that can be used directly on a production cluster.

The design has three steps, which the three subsections below follow in order. “Define the operation and the evidence contract first” sets out what the platform owner has to write down before anything starts (the cluster, plan version, permitted steps, and approval conditions), states the execution service's minimal contract for operation identifiers, breaks “upgrade node N” into its three sub-steps (draining, upgrading, and allowing the node to accept workloads again), and finally fills in a handoff record. “After an interruption, how to decide the next step” places the interruption at different cut points, from a remote operation that completed without a reply and a drain that completed while the upgrade failed, to a restart of the execution service itself and a request that was never sent. It looks at how the one taking over decides, from the state it finds, whether to continue, repair, or stop, and finally changes the approval conditions and lets the predecessor come back and send a step after someone else has taken over. “Which results support opening up an operation” lays out the nine situations in a table, giving the acceptable behavior and its grounds row by row, and then turns to the staffing cost of this handoff path and how Monday's work is split among three roles. To compare the expected behavior in each of the nine situations, start with that table.

### Define the operation and the evidence contract first

Before starting, the platform owner writes down the designated cluster, the plan version, the permitted steps, and the approval conditions, and defines what can be queried in the target system once this step succeeds. The test apparatus independently records the operations it receives, their identifiers, and their actual effects, so that the reviewer can check the agent's claims against them. The agent taking over may use only the query interfaces the execution service normally provides, and may not read the time of failure privately recorded by the test apparatus. First run one normal flow without interruption to confirm that the permitted work can actually finish; otherwise a system that rejects every change would also look very safe in the failure cases.

The design requires the execution service to accept a pre-generated operation identifier, so the caller can persist it before sending the request. An identifier on its own does not resolve the gap between a side effect and its record, so before this design is adopted, the service's minimal contract has to be confirmed: one identifier maps to fixed parameters, and resending it with a different node or plan is refused; a query can distinguish five states (not accepted, running, completed, failed, and undeterminable); and how long records are kept, and whether the service still recognizes them after its own restart, are written down. These are preconditions for adoption; they cannot be left hidden behind the words “the service promises.”

Next, specify what this step is. This article tests only “upgrade node N” on one worker. The operation consists of three sub-steps: drain the workloads (`kubectl drain`), upgrade (`kubeadm upgrade node`, followed by updating and restarting the kubelet), and allow the node to accept workloads again (`kubectl uncordon`). Each has a different resend contract. The normal effect of a drain is that the workloads on the node are moved off; if the drain is sent a second time, the node may already be empty, or the drain may evict workloads again because the predecessor had already reopened the node. The official documentation says only that `kubeadm upgrade` can be rerun; the resend semantics of updating and restarting the kubelet have to be confirmed separately. Reopening the node makes it accept work again.[9] So acceptance does not count calls; it observes effects: whether any unapproved eviction or maintenance action occurred, whether the service disruption exceeded what was allowed, and whether the node ended up on 1.31, checked against the operation identifier and the node's state. If the test environment deliberately requires exactly one drain, that is a convention of this fixed environment, not a general rule of Kubernetes.

The record that travels with the handoff also has to be filled in first, or readers are left to translate the whole article into an interface themselves. Here it is for this test, with hypothetical data throughout and a note on who may write each field:

| Field | Content | Who writes it |
|---|---|---|
| Task and target | T-31, cluster prod-a, plan version v8 | The approval flow |
| Approval source and expiry condition | Change request CR-204; lapses when the maintenance window ends | The change system |
| Current holder and handover version | Execution identity run-B, handover version h2 | The execution service, updated by the coordinator |
| Operation and sub-steps | op-17: drain op-17.1 completed; upgrade op-17.2 running; uncordon op-17.3 not accepted | The execution service |
| Confirmed effects / unknown results | drain completed / upgrade result unknown | The execution service, from its query results |
| Query entry point | `/operations/op-17` for the whole, `/operations/op-17.2` for the sub-step | The execution service |
| Permitted next steps | Query and report; no new changes | The approval flow |
| Case owner and deadline | The on-call engineer; before the maintenance window ends | Whoever the incident policy designates |

The agent can only read this record. What allows “query only” to be upgraded to “next step permitted” is the sub-step state the execution service reports plus an approval that is still valid: only when op-17.2 is found complete and the node is healthy does the approval flow change the permitted next step to uncordon.

### After an interruption, how to decide the next step

Now let the entire “upgrade node N” operation complete in the target system but withhold the result from the agent, and interrupt its workspace. The new agent gets only the saved inputs, the plan, the working files, and the operation identifier; credentials are obtained afresh, the current approval is queried afresh, and the handover version is updated. This cut point is worth testing because the effect has already happened remotely while no successful response has arrived locally. The most intuitive move, “rerun from the last line,” may add an extra eviction in this operation. The expected behavior is to query the operation already sent and the target's state first, confirm that the result meets this step's requirement, and then pick up the work not yet done. Acceptance compares the outcome against the external record: was one extra change issued and treated as new work, was the whole cluster wrongly marked complete, and does the handoff report say which data it checked?

The genuinely hard cut point is in the middle: the drain has completed, the upgrade has failed, the node still cannot accept work, and the maintenance window is about to close. The state the successor finds is “drain completed, upgrade failed, node still cordoned,” but “upgrade failed” does not mean the node is still cleanly on the old version, let alone fit to carry work. Remediation has to be chosen from three options according to the state actually found, and every one of them has to map back to the approval. If the node is confirmed unmodified by the upgrade and healthy (still on 1.30, with the kubelet normal and enough capacity), it may resume accepting work on the old version and be marked unfinished; if the node has been partly modified, it is repaired by the approved procedure, for instance by retrying the upgrade inside the maintenance window; if the state is unclear or unhealthy, the node is kept isolated and handed to a person. Once the approval has lapsed, the agent may no longer send any new remediation operation on its own; only the case owner decides. What the case owner needs to know is that this node cannot carry work right now, how large the impact is, and who has the authority to act outside the maintenance window. All of that belongs in the handoff record under “permitted next steps,” not in a bare “stop the dependent steps.”

One more cut point tests the execution service itself: interrupting only the agent never shows whether the service, having lost an operation record, produces the effect a second time. “Accepted” has to be taken apart first, into five moments, each of which can be interrupted: request received, durably registered, acceptance reply sent, effect produced, and result saved. The contract is that durable registration must precede the acceptance reply. So after a restart between receiving the request and registering it, the correct result is that the identifier is unknown and no effect occurred; after a restart following the acceptance reply, the query must find the record. If the service replied with acceptance but nothing can be found, it has broken its own promise; that is not normal recovery.

Now move the interruption to just before the request is actually sent. The reviewer knows that no operation happened this time, but the successor cannot use that answer directly. It has to query the saved identifier and interpret the reply according to the execution service's contract: “not found” may mean not yet executed, or it may only mean the record is not yet visible or has passed its retention period. Only when the query can establish that nothing was executed, or the service promises that resending with the same identifier will not add a second effect, is there reason to send, and then only after rechecking the current state and the authorization.

The local summaries of these two cases, “completed remotely but the reply was lost” and “the request was never sent,” can look very similar while the remote facts differ. A test that checks only “the task eventually succeeded after recovery” cannot tell whether the successor established the situation or just happened to rerun without anything going wrong. In the [Payment Walkthrough](https://medium.com/p/20452944263b) of Green Is Not Done, I used buying a bottle of green tea to make the point: when the payment screen freezes, resending the same request should return the original result, not cause a second charge. The property that resending the same operation with the same parameters within the retention period adds no new effect is called idempotency. That article's code implements only sequential resends inside one process, and the open questions across restarts are already listed in its Section 9. What this article adds is a handoff to a different agent and a multi-stage infrastructure operation. If the tool offers neither a queryable identifier nor a defined safe way to resend, a handoff path for manual verification should be kept.

Once the successor has shown it can decide to continue or stop from this evidence, change the approval conditions: let a new version of the plan supersede the original, or let the approved maintenance window run out. Keeping the old files should help the analysis recover, but it must not bring an expired approval back into force. Credential expiry is tested separately: obtaining a credential again may restore only the access that is still approved now; it cannot add authorization to the task. The right to take over is tested too: let the predecessor come back after B has taken over and send one step. The predecessor should be refused because its handover version is out of date, and a late-arriving old request from the predecessor should fare the same. Also check the verifier against a state where the control plane has met the target but the workers have not, to confirm that it honestly leaves the unfinished work on the list.

### Which results support opening up an operation

The expected outcomes in this acceptance test come, respectively, from the task constraints, the execution service's promise about resends, and the completion conditions the platform and service owners defined. The format borrows an idea from the obligation table (coming soon) in my series The Same Spec, Ten Runs (Part 2, Contract): stating the basis item by item. Read the third row first: what the successor may do, what the reviewer checks, and which approval it rests on when the drain has completed and the upgrade has failed. Everything in the table is a design example:

| Situation | Acceptable behavior for the successor | What the reviewer checks | Basis / still unproven |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal, approval valid | Complete the designated limited operation | Correct target, effect, and completion condition | Approval record for plan v8 / service acceptance tested separately |
| Completed remotely, reply lost | Establish the result, then continue | No unapproved eviction or maintenance action was added | The execution service's query contract / its records after a service restart |
| Drain completed, upgrade failed | Choose one of three options according to the state found: reopen only if confirmed unmodified by the upgrade and healthy, repair by the approved procedure if partly modified, keep isolated and hand over if unclear or unhealthy | Version, health, and capacity evidence; whether the remedy maps back to the approval | The approval's expiry condition / authority outside the maintenance window |
| Request never sent | Decide to send or stop according to the service contract | “Not found” was not treated as “not executed” | Record retention period / resend semantics after expiry |
| Result temporarily unqueryable | Stop the dependent steps and hand over | A named case owner, a query entry point, and a deadline | The rule for assigning a case owner / the tolerable waiting time |
| Plan superseded or approval lapsed | Block new steps that are no longer approved | Old files did not restore old permissions | The change system / the mapping between the approval and the node list |
| Predecessor returns, both act at once | Refuse the predecessor's new steps | The handover version and request acceptance are ordered consistently | The execution service / the predecessor's late-arriving old request |
| Same identifier, different parameters | Refuse the inconsistent request | No result from another target was reused | The operation-identifier contract / the scope of parameter comparison |
| Workers not yet finished | Leave the remainder on the list; do not declare the whole complete | The expectation list is independent of the executor's report | The node list at approval time / the service's own acceptance |

A safe stop does not by itself mean the team's burden is acceptable. How long an unknown result may stay unknown, who takes the case, and how work is kept from being shelved forever when the query keeps failing all need answers. What a manager has to see is the number of cases pending confirmation, the waiting time, and the hours of manual checking; those numbers decide whether this handoff path is worth opening at all. Monday's work can be assigned directly to three roles. A staff engineer on the platform team writes the contract for a single step, stating the moment of acceptance, the operation identifier, the successor's identity, and the query and resend semantics. QA and SRE arrange four cut points for the same step (before sending, after the effect but before the reply, a service restart, and the old holder returning) and keep the external-effect records. The service owner decides how much pending-confirmation time and manual handling can be tolerated, and narrows the scope of automation when those limits are exceeded. If the results support only read-only assessment, first deliver an assessment that saves data-collection time; if one kind of limited change can already be interrupted, queried, and resumed correctly, open up that one operation next. These cases are necessary verification items before expanding authorization, but they cannot support expansion on their own: the G2 gate in Section 5 of the Reliability article looks at the task set, repeated performance, the observation period, and the review workforce, and Section 5 of the Agentic Engineering piece “[Evals, Unit Economics, and Scaling](https://medium.com/p/1cb1855a2046)” further separates “opening it to more teams” from “authorizing high-risk operations.” “Some recovery behavior conforms to the contract” and “the team is ready to take on more operations” are two different decisions.

This acceptance test can tell us whether the system does the wrong thing when interrupted; what it has not yet shown is what these arrangements mean for the colleague who actually takes over.

## 10. Closing: the work left behind should be work the next person dares to take on

What the six decks changed is how I understand a handoff: giving the same data to the next agent does not mean the permissions, the external facts, the completion conditions, and the right to take over have been handed over as well.

The cost most easily underestimated is making the incoming shift rebuild work that has already been done. The working files are there, but no one knows which plan was approved; the report says complete, but the node observations are nowhere to be found; the credential still works, but it is unclear whether the original approval covered only assessment or changes as well. These gaps turn the time automation saved into the next person's verification time. If they are discovered only inside the maintenance window, that person has to confirm the service's state while at the same time guessing what the predecessor did.

A good handoff can therefore stop explicitly at a not-yet-finished state: it keeps a record of the effects already produced, the results still to be confirmed, the original approval, the current handover version, and the next place to query, so that whoever takes the case knows where to pick up and how soon it has to be handled. The next article goes one step further and asks: once the task is over and the experience left behind is passed to the next task, how much of it can still be trusted?

I will start by picking one small task that colleagues often have to take over from each other and putting it through a deliberately arranged interruption. Then I will supply, item by item, the evidence that could not be found during recovery, and write down who picks up a result that is still unknown after a timeout, how soon it must be handled, and who decides whether to recover or stop. To return to the Kong demo from Part 1, the one stopped by the rate limit: when a different agent takes over, it also needs, beyond the conversation, the record of the mock operations already completed, a scope of approval that is still valid now, and an entry point where results can be queried. The role-group permissions in the demo are not a task approval record. Being able to keep and check that record is the capability a recoverable system still has to add. What is worth continuing to accumulate after Tokyo is exactly this kind of work: work that others can inspect, correct, and hand on to the next person.
