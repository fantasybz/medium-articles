# Before One Agent Hands Work to Another: Authority, State, and Recovery

> **TL;DR** — When one agent hands work to another, or a workspace restarts and a different agent takes over, the predecessor may have been cut off, or it may come back later; on what grounds does the next one obtain the right to take over, and decide whether to continue, redo, or stop? A handoff can carry the earlier conversation, the working files, even a claim of “approved”; but the transfer itself does not guarantee that any of it is complete, trustworthy, or still valid. What the successor has to recheck is: whether the originally approved scope is still valid now, who is entitled to add operations at this moment, how far the remote system actually got, and what is still missing before the work counts as done. This article follows one hypothetical Kubernetes upgrade through six Tokyo decks, to see which kind of evidence each supplies to the successor. My judgment: a handoff must carry a task scope and a progress record that can be checked, authorization must be rechecked at the moment of action, and completion must be confirmed against the external system. Protocols let components interoperate; those three things remain the responsibility of the whole system.

## 2. At a handoff, what exactly is handed over?

Start with a shift handover. The person going off duty leaves a note: “Machine A done, B not yet.” What the person coming on duty really needs to ask comes down to four things: whose permissions did you use to do that, whose turn is it to take over now, where exactly has the system got to, and what is still missing before we can close out? The note answers half of the third question. A handoff between agents is the same, except that the note has become a conversation log and a working directory.

Handoff situations are best separated with two questions. The first: is control being handed over, or is work merely being farmed out while you wait for the result? When a call is transferred and the refund desk carries on with the customer while the original person withdraws, that is the former, which OpenAI's guide to building agents calls the decentralized pattern; when a manager sends someone to look something up and the result comes back to the manager, that is the latter, the manager pattern.[4] OpenAI's Agents SDK (a development kit) implements the former as a tool call: the new agent gets the earlier conversation by default, and the developer can filter what it sees, or have code check the order before the baton passes; the details are in the reference.[3] One subagent design described in an Anthropic engineering article leans toward the latter: a subagent receives only its own task package, never the manager's full conversation, and hands back a condensed summary.[5] The second question: does the handoff cross a service boundary, or run into a restart? Delegation across services is like an assessor handing later execution to an execution service maintained by another team; taking over after a restart means the predecessor's workspace is gone and the new agent gets only the files it left behind. The two questions combine freely; the SDK's switch is not a cross-service protocol and provides neither durable storage nor failure recovery, and this article compares the evidence every combination needs.

Put them together and what a handoff can actually pass along is the conversation and its state, sometimes in condensed form. Approval records, operation results and acceptance conditions can travel with it too, but the transfer itself does not guarantee that they are complete, trustworthy, or still valid. The conversation log may say “approved,” but that is the previous agent's claim. In Section 6 of the [Review article](https://medium.com/p/4d36d0f2f9c1) in Green Is Not Done, I wrote that a “pre-approved” in a pull request (PR) description has to be looked up in the approval record, not read off the description; a handoff message is the same kind of input.

One concrete task runs through the rest of this article. Kubernetes is a system that coordinates how applications run across a group of machines; that group is called a cluster, and each machine a node. A platform engineer asks an agent to assess whether a particular cluster can be upgraded from 1.30 to 1.31. What is handed over at first is only the assessment; changing any node is not allowed. Suppose assessment and remediation are finished and a person approves a plan; only then does an execution agent begin working through the nodes one at a time. After the request for one step has been sent, the workspace restarts. A new agent takes over the directory and finds the progress note its predecessor left: “upgrade in progress.” This is the hypothetical case that ties the sessions together; it is not an experiment run at the venue.

I place the handoff at this moment deliberately, because “request sent” cannot answer what the incoming shift cares about most. The working directory may be preserved intact while the answers remain incomplete. If the next agent receives nothing more than “finish the upgrade,” it may use its own broader permissions to supply authorization the original task never granted, or treat its predecessor's plan as something that has already happened. One more situation has to be in from the start: A was only disconnected, B has already taken over, and A comes back later; both hold an approval that is still valid, so who may send the next step? Section 7 handles that question and Section 9 tests it. The reading map for this article follows; the six sessions give the four questions a basis that can be checked:

## 3. Once a conversation is allowed, who gets to decide the next action?

Ryuji Iijima of SoftBank starts “Designing Trust Boundaries for Agent-to-Agent Systems” from a proof of concept and asks where identity, messages, and permitted actions should be checked when several agents work together ([official agenda](https://agntconmcpconjapan26.sched.com/event/2RCwp/designing-trust-boundaries-for-agent-to-agent-systems-lessons-learned-from-a-technical-poc-ryuji-iijima-softbank-corp), [talk recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IO6CFh_lRGw), [public slides](https://hosted-files.sched.co/agntconmcpconjapan26/3a/AGNTCon_MCPCon_Japan2026%E7%99%BB%E5%A3%87%E8%B3%87%E6%96%99_SoftBank_Corp_%E9%A3%AF%E5%B3%B6%E7%AB%9C%E5%85%90.pdf)). This session answers the first question of the handover: being able to talk to you is not the same as being able to act with your permissions.

The deck begins with an Agent Firewall. In one sentence, what it checks is this: A may contact B, but whether B may act with its own account still has to be checked separately; the firewall controls which agents may talk to each other and whether what they say gets through. The Agent-to-Agent (A2A) protocol gives different agents a common way to exchange tasks and messages, like team members handing work to one another; the Model Context Protocol (MCP) handles the interface through which an agent uses external tools and data, like each member using the company's systems. The PoC uses A2A v0.3, and page 6 limits its scope of observation to communication between agents; MCP tool execution, agents' access to data, and a complete security operations platform stay out of scope. Separating these stretches of the path is what gives the later questions a definite target.[6]

### Check communication permission separately from authority to act

His first lesson is one of definition: A2A is only one way agents interact, not what defines an agent. He defines an agent as something that “makes decisions and takes actions”; some agents never speak A2A yet can change external systems all the same. From that definition he goes on to point out: A2A tells us how agents communicate, not where trust should end. Even if A may talk to B, B may still hold its own enterprise accounts, APIs, and data-access permissions, and one permitted message can go on to trigger an action that was never inside A2A's inspection scope. Page 11 asks directly: can an action that falls outside A2A's scope be trusted? His answer is “of course not.”[6]

📌【在此插入圖 asset-02.png】

Ryuji Iijima, SoftBank, slide page 12: the A2A communication boundary and a trust boundary designed around decisions, authority, and actions. Source: public conference slides; original image retained.


Back in the upgrade case, the assessment agent may ask the execution agent to “confirm whether this plan is feasible.” That does not authorize it to drain a node, that is, to move the application workloads that can be moved off that machine first so it can be maintained. If the execution agent holds broader permissions and treats the text it received as full approval, “who may contact me” has turned into “whose full power may be used.” The problem is that the downstream side supplied permissions the request never carried; confirming that both ends are genuine is not enough to decide this action.

A handoff therefore has to let the downstream side recover at least the original task, the delegator, the target, and the scope of operations, and those conditions have to trace back to an authorization source that can be verified. If an agent can add “approved” to its own message and the next service simply complies, adding a field only swaps a self-declaration in natural language for a self-declaration in a structured one. Authority is looked up, not read off; the Review article cited in Section 2 was about PR descriptions, and what is added here is where the check sits: at the operation boundary, not where the message comes in. A model may propose the assessment result; the decision that grants execution rights must be made by a person or system entitled to make it, and checked at the operation boundary. This is a design requirement I extend from the talk.

### Semantic inspection must also decide when to wait, reject, or hand over to a person

Page 13 separates two kinds of check: the connection check decides whether A may talk to B at all, and the content check decides whether this conversation should continue. Take the same request: whether the target node is on the approved list can be settled by comparison, and that is a deterministic rule; whether the message text smuggles in an instruction that changes the task can only be known by understanding its meaning, and that is semantic inspection, where model judgment comes in. This order keeps the reproducible, lower-cost controls and places the more expensive, less stable judgment where meaning genuinely has to be understood. The design principle on pages 16 and 17 is “Rule-based by default; model-based semantic inspection only where necessary,” with one more line: “Do NOT turn every control policy intent into a prompt. Turn it into a rule first, unless meaning requires a model.”[6]

Why semantic inspection is unstable can be understood by thinking of reading a letter. When a message arrives piece by piece, that is, when it is streamed, the first half of a sentence may describe a dangerous command and only the second half say “do not run it”; judging each fragment on its own the moment it arrives blocks it wrongly. Waiting for more content adds latency. Page 15 lists when to aggregate, how much context is enough, false blocks and misses, and what to do when the judgment fails as problems on the same control path, and states plainly that “Judgment is not fully reproducible” and “Violations cannot be detected 100%.”

📌【在此插入圖 asset-03.png】

SoftBank, slide page 15: streamed content needs enough context to form first, and semantic judgment also has to handle latency, misjudgment, and failure. Source: public conference slides.


So I would not only ask “is there a safety model?” I would ask: can an action be triggered while the content is still undecided; when the judgment service times out, does the system wait, reject, or hand over to a person; and which kind of error is worth which kind of cost. Wrongly blocking an explanatory passage may cost only a wait; missing an unauthorized node drain affects the workloads. These conditions should be defined by the service owner, not left to the model to decide how much risk is acceptable.

It also matters whether the check happens before or after the effect. In the upgrade case: if what is checked is a request that has not yet reached the execution service, blocking it prevents the drain; if what is checked is the report streaming back after the operation has started, blocking the reply cannot undo a drain that has already happened, and all it can do is detect and start recovery. Waiting has a cost too: while the judgment is pending, the request holds a connection and time. My handling: when semantic judgment times out, accept no new drains, where “new” is counted from the moment the execution service durably registers a request, so anything registered before then counts as new; registered operations keep being tracked; and requests still queued after the check whose approval has since expired are not executed. The cost is waiting and human intervention, and the service owner decides how much of it to bear. This is my derivation along the case; SoftBank's PoC kept its scope of observation at the communication layer and did not validate complete operation control.

SoftBank's conclusion is that “The protocol stopped being the boundary of what we needed to govern”: the scope of control widens from the communication path to the entity that makes decisions. The next question is how to write “how far this task may go” as a limit that actually takes effect.

**After a handoff, an action that will actually change external state still has to be checked against the original task's authorization at the operation boundary; an “approved” in the message is only where the check begins.**

## 4. Turning this task's boundaries into limits that actually take effect

Masaya Nakamura of Studist presses a question in “Intent as Code”: if an account has the right to operate a system, is that enough to define what the agent may do this time? He uses open-source tools to show how targets and conditions can be written as rules a program can check ([official agenda](https://agntconmcpconjapan26.sched.com/event/2QlDX/intent-as-code-why-existing-permissions-arent-enough-for-ai-masaya-nakamura-studist), [public slides](https://hosted-files.sched.co/agntconmcpconjapan26/ab/Intent-as-Code%20%2813%29.pdf)). This session answers the other half of the first question: the permissions are there; where is the scope of this task?

His central distinction can be understood through an access badge. An engineer's badge opens every door in the company, but this task only needs to get into one meeting room. What the account can do is the larger set, and the operations the task allows are usually a small part of it; he calls the former permission (what can be done) and the latter intent (how far this task may go), and points out that the risk lies exactly where “what can be executed” exceeds “what may be executed this time.” Page 33 adds a design principle: letting the model make a non-deterministic judgment should be the last line of defense.[7]

### Shrink account capabilities to the operations this task allows

The deck lists four risk examples; I take the third in full, because it can change the automated process that runs afterward. First, the everyday meaning of a few words: after changing code, an engineer saves a version locally (a commit) and then sends it to the remote code repository (a push to the repo); the remote then automatically runs builds and tests, a process called CI, whose definition is itself a file kept in the repository. An agent that can push can therefore change the CI configuration in passing and have CI do, for it, whatever the CI account and the passwords or API keys it can reach for logging into other systems (its secrets) allow: while fixing code, the agent changes the process that checks its own work. The other three examples, issues edited or leaked by mistake, external code loaded during a Terraform preview, and instructions hidden in a web page, are in the notes at the end.

His countermeasures are a handful of CLI wrapper tools, that is, a layer of checking wrapped around the original command, which the same operating command passes through before it is sent. All of the following are the example configurations on the slides: `safe-gh` allows only reads against allowlisted organizations, and the issue-editing rule in the demo requires an issue in the designated organization, authored by oneself and carrying an AI annotation, while creating issues, commenting and other operations are not shown; with a shared account “authored by oneself” is not the same as “created by this agent,” and an issue the AI created may have nothing to do with the current task, so these rules first shrink the set of possible operations, and restricting them to “this task” still needs a separate check that the issue belongs to the task. `safe-push` inspects the committed files before a push, refuses or asks for human approval when they touch CI definitions such as `.github/`, keeps a human `--force` path, and restricts the remote target; a path check protects the listed scope, while a script CI loads from some other path can still change CI's behavior, so a complete check has to follow the dependency path, at the cost of maintenance and false blocks. `safe-webfetch` handles reading web pages, see the notes. Their shared direction is to define the target, the operation, and the conditions first, then have a program check them, so that a request outside those conditions is blocked before it actually executes.[7] Back in the upgrade case: the assessment may read cluster versions and resources, but that does not grant it permission to drain, modify, or delete nodes; once the work reaches the stage where the cluster has to change, the execution service should check the target, steps, and conditions approved for this task. This guards against more than malicious content; even an agent trying in good faith to finish the job can mistake “this change is faster” for “I may do this while I am at it.”

### Put the enforcing check where the agent cannot route around it

Where a limit sits, though, decides how much responsibility it can carry. The wrapper is the layer just described; a hook inserts a check at a specified moment. Page 58 points straight at the limit of such local controls: if the agent can still call the original CLI or API, obtain the same credentials, or even rewrite the restriction settings, the extra wrapped check can be bypassed. His conclusion is to enforce the boundary outside the environment the agent can operate on: page 59 moves the operations that need permissions into a managed remote execution environment, which verifies the target and conditions, uses the necessary permissions, and returns the result; the credentials and the judgment of “may this run” are separated out of the agent's workspace.[7]

📌【在此插入圖 asset-04.png】

Masaya Nakamura, Studist, slide page 58: local wrappers and hooks can still leave bypass paths; enforcement has to sit in an environment the agent cannot modify. Source: public conference slides; original image in Japanese.


This changed how I would accept “a hook has been added.” The check should follow every path that can reach the target: after the restricted tool refuses, can the direct API still complete the same operation? Can the agent change the control file and call again? The key is not where the machine is but three things: can the agent modify the policy, can it obtain the underlying credentials, and can it bypass the checking entry point. The same machine can hold controls the agent has no right to modify, and a remote service can also accept requests that are far too permissive. Section 6 of [The Harness Blueprint](https://medium.com/p/3facc281f633) already required secrets held on the tool side, a short-lived, narrowly scoped credential for each run (one execution of the agent, from launch to finish), outbound connections denied by default, and paths that cannot be bypassed; the agent holds only a key reference, the tool side still verifies who may use it when the reference arrives, and holding the reference does not mean the key can be obtained. What this article adds is the part that crosses runs: once the same task has changed to a new run, the operation identifier must stay the same, the identity of the one taking over changes, and the old run loses the right to add operations. Walk the path once and it is clear: the agent hands over a request to “process node N,” the entry point that receives requests (the gateway) checks the task and the target, the tool runs in an isolated environment that opens only the necessary directories and connections (the sandbox), and the key it needs is fetched at the moment of execution by a service that fetches it on its behalf (the secret broker); from start to finish the agent touches neither the rules it has to obey nor the key.

For this upgrade, one design worth discussing is an execution service that accepts a limited request of the form “process this node under this version of the plan,” checks the plan against the current conditions, and only then acts with its own restricted credentials. The agent never needs a credential that can change the whole cluster at will. Where the task rules come from, and how they map to an approved version, also has to be settled: the plan version and the permitted steps are written into the execution service by the approval flow, and the agent's request can only reference them, never carry its own; moving the policy to a remote service only changes who can modify it, and does not automatically make the policy correct. The cost is that the execution service must spell out which steps it supports and how it reports errors, and someone has to maintain it as operations are added; the more general the operation interface, the more likely it re-exposes arbitrary execution, and the more specialized, the more maintenance and exception handling it needs. I would draw the line at operations that recur within the task and whose effect can be described, and leave the rest to people; what it buys is a boundary that can be checked directly. This is my design suggestion, not a Kubernetes implementation the deck delivered.

### Use permitted and out-of-bounds requests to check that the limit takes effect

To verify, first send one request that matches the approval, then keep every other condition the same and change only the target to an unapproved node, or only the step to an operation not in the plan. The first should proceed; the other two should be rejected before any external effect occurs. Only then do you know the limit rests on the task scope rather than merely recognizing this account. A local wrapper can still flag common mistakes early and keep developers off the failure path; it is just that when the same executor can rewrite it, the final enforcement has to be carried by another boundary.

These limits give the work an enforceable boundary, but they leave another problem: even when no operation exceeds its authority, the system may act on a stale assessment and do the wrong thing in the right format. The Kubernetes session puts “may execute” and “suitable to execute now” into the same workflow.

**Whether a task limit is effective depends on whether every path that can reach an external operation makes its allow-or-reject decision from the same authorized scope.**

## 5. Recommending, approving, and accepting an upgrade need different evidence

Sanskar Agrawalla and Abhijeet Chaudhuri of Quartic.ai put an agent inside a cluster upgrade workflow in “Letting an Agent Upgrade Production Kubernetes,” working in order through collecting data, assessing compatibility, obtaining approval, executing, and verifying ([official agenda](https://agntconmcpconjapan26.sched.com/event/2QlD9/letting-an-agent-upgrade-production-kubernetes-without-getting-paged-at-3-am-sanskar-agrawalla-abhijeet-chaudhuri-quarticai), [public slides](https://hosted-files.sched.co/agntconmcpconjapan26/d9/AGNTCon-MCPCon-Japan-2026_Abhijeet_Sanskar_final.pdf)). This session answers the fourth question of the handover: what counts as done, and it learned the answer from a failure.

Their demonstration environment is a local test cluster built with kind, with the model served through a local Ollama; page 17 states this clearly. kind is a tool for building a test cluster on a local machine, and Ollama is a service for running models locally; the deck does not say which model version was used.[8] The upgrade itself is a single command. The hard part is everything that has to be confirmed before that command: removed APIs, the compatibility of the various add-ons and components, whether the backup can be restored, and the conditions for rollback. Their question is direct: what has to hold before an agent may execute? Page 11 gives five answers: evidence over opinion, machine-readable decisions, a not-recommended result is a hard stop, irreversible actions need human approval, and completion must be independently verified.

### An assessment that says feasible still needs approval to execute

What makes this case most worth reading is that it does not compress the upgrade into one big prompt. In the flow on page 14, a data collector first gathers cluster data, rules check it, and a model assesses it and produces a report; the checkpoint that decides whether the flow may continue is called a gate, and only past it does the work move on to remediation and execution, with a verification component confirming the result at the end. Each step answers a different question and also limits what the previous step's answer can stand for. The division of labor on page 13: the agent decides what to do next; the system decides what it is allowed to do. Why is a model needed at all? Rules can check known conditions; compatibility notes, change logs and vendor announcements are unstructured text, and judging “does this document cover our configuration” is where a model earns its place. When no compatibility evidence can be found, the model may not make up an answer; it can only report the gap, which is why the UNKNOWN state below exists. Compared with a fully fixed automated flow, what the model saves is the time people spend reading documents, and the price is that its judgment has to be framed by the assessment states and the approval.

The data collector can only observe. Page 19 puts `get`, `version`, `top`, `api-resources`, and `cluster-info` on a read-only list and rejects mutating operations at the function boundary. Next come pure Python rules, checking the known conditions that make upgrades fail: an old deployment format that the new version no longer accepts (removed APIs), an add-on version that does not yet support the target version (a vendor support matrix encoded as rules), incompatible controllers, webhooks that would block the process, and version gaps. Take one request to create a workload to see the last two words: the request first reaches the cluster's front desk (the API server); if it matches a configured rule, the front desk first calls an external checking service, and that call point is an admission webhook; once it is let through, the program that continually adjusts actual state toward what the configuration asks for (the controller) takes over. If the request must pass that checking service, the service does not support the new version, and it is configured to reject on failure, then after the upgrade this kind of new workload cannot be created; a controller on an incompatible version may stop adjusting. An upgrade has to confirm beforehand that these interdependent components can still work together. The rules output counts, not prose; and “Unverified compatibility is treated as risk, not as silence.”

Page 21 parses the assessment result into four states: `APPROVED` still needs human approval; `CONDITIONAL` needs remediation first, or a person accepting an explicit, bounded risk; `NOT RECOMMENDED` blocks execution; and when the data or output cannot be reliably interpreted the state is `UNKNOWN`, which likewise may not proceed. The slide's title is “The Model Can Recommend. It Cannot Authorize.” At the end there is one more hard stop: the agent pauses and asks “continue with the upgrade?”, and an explicit answer is required; y executes, n stops the upgrade.

📌【在此插入圖 asset-05.png】

Sanskar Agrawalla and Abhijeet Chaudhuri, Quartic.ai, slide page 21: assessment state is kept separate from human approval, and UNKNOWN cannot be treated as a pass. Source: public conference slides.


The two most important positions in the figure are that “assessment passed” is still followed by a decision, and that “cannot interpret” has an exit of its own. The three gates in Section 6 of the Green Is Not Done overview check test discrimination, review and task reliability, and the Testing article already required that an unexpected failure not count as a pass; this session is another application of the same principle, turning upgrade readiness into four machine-readable states. What this article really adds is the question that follows: after a handoff, how do the assessment, the approval and the current state get realigned.

### After remediation, reconfirm the assessment and the approval

Why reassess after remediation? Because the original report describes the cluster before the repair. Replacing one deprecated API may change how a controller is deployed at the same time; the other conditions that held before do not automatically keep holding because that one item turned green. Page 22 requires going back to assessment after remediation, with “no bypass, no ‘continue first and see.’”

For example, incomplete compatibility data for a webhook and a confirmed finding that the webhook does not support the target version are two different reasons to block. The first calls for finding the maintainer or obtaining verifiable data; the second calls for scheduling a fix and then observing again. If both are collapsed into one low confidence score, the person taking over can hardly tell whether to gather data or change the system. The value of keeping the assessment state is that the next piece of work has a direction, and human approval can then be aimed at a concrete plan and the remaining risk.

I would extend this design one step further: the approval record should map to a specific cluster, a specific plan version, and a specific observation of the environment. This is my design suggestion; the slides do not show that complete approval binding and expiry mechanisms have been implemented. The environment keeps changing after approval, but not every change calls for a new signature, or the flow stalls; carrying the approval forward regardless means executing a stale plan. I would sort changes into three classes: observation updates that do not affect the approval, for instance a new health check whose result matches the earlier one, which is merely recorded; changes that need reassessment but can keep the approved scope, for instance a target node's resource usage fluctuating within the range the approval stated, where the assessment is rerun and the approved targets and steps stay; and changes that need fresh approval, for instance an added node, a swapped controller, a different plan regenerated by the agent, or a changed target version. The contract I adopt here is that the approval binds to a fixed list of node identities, and an added node means revising the scope. The alternative is to approve a dynamic set defined by explicit conditions, which then has to be bounded separately by role, count, capacity and maintenance limits, with any added node having to prove it meets them. Either works; what does not is demanding that approval bind to targets while quietly letting “same role” widen them. The variation in observations comes in three kinds: state transitions the plan expects, such as a node's version reaching 1.31 in sequence, which do not affect the approval; variation within the pre-approved bounds, such as resource usage fluctuating within the stated range, where reassessment is enough; and variation beyond the bounds, such as the node list, roles, controller or webhook versions, or the target version changing, where the approval lapses. So what the approval has to state is not just a snapshot but the conditions of acceptable variation; before the next operation, the execution service checks them, rather than the agent describing them itself. When acceptance is declared there is one more inconsistency to handle: if the node list at approval time does not match the current node list, completion cannot be declared. It cannot leave behind a bare `approved: true` for every later version to inherit. This is exactly the “which plan version the approval covers” in the first row of the table in Section 2.

### Accept layer by layer, from every node to the service outcome

Once the approved list is fixed, who confirms that the executor did not miss one of the nodes? Page 29 candidly records a failure: the first version of the logic looked only at the first node and treated the whole cluster as upgraded. A cluster has two roles: control plane nodes coordinate the cluster, and workers carry the application workloads. In a case with three control plane nodes and three workers, the control plane had reached 1.31 while the workers were still on 1.30, yet that hop was marked a success. After the fix, the executor finds every node by role, processes them in order, and the next version step does not start until every node is in place. Their conclusion: “A cluster upgrade succeeds only when the whole cluster has crossed the version boundary.”[8]

📌【在此插入圖 asset-06.png】

Quartic.ai, slide page 29: the first version checked only the first node; the fix identifies node roles, upgrades node by node, and confirms every node before the next version step. Source: public conference slides.


I borrowed this failure in Section 5 of the [Testing article](https://medium.com/p/51d001a6dcd5) in Green Is Not Done, where the point was that the checker itself has to be checked: a check that samples only part of the boundary reports green. Placed in a handoff, it carries one more layer of meaning. If the executor misses the workers, and the verifier checks only the nodes the executor reports as “the ones I processed,” the two miss the same set of targets together. The source of the acceptance list therefore matters: my verification design would build its expectations from the cluster inventory and target requirements at the time of approval, then independently query each node's identity, role, and version. One can deliberately leave one target worker on the old version while every other node meets the goal; if the check still declares completion, it is verifying the successes it happened to see. After the fix, keep one normal case where everything meets the goal too, to confirm that the check does not become so conservative that it can never finish. The Testing article said the checker has to be checked; here it goes one step further: the acceptance list may not inherit the executor's omissions.

Once the successor knows which nodes remain, it still has to ask whether the tools in hand can continue the original plan. Page 30 records that an intended upgrade from 1.30 to 1.31 pulled in version 1.32 of kubeadm, the upgrade tool, because the package was not pinned; the 1.32 tool refused to upgrade a 1.30 control plane, and the upgrade stopped before it began. The fix was to pin the tool version for each hop and clear out stale package sources. Their lesson: the cluster version is not the only version that has to be controlled; the upgrade tool, too, has to move one minor version at a time.[8] Kubernetes' own rules likewise require one minor version per hop.[9] For the successor this adds one more thing to hand over: which tool version the predecessor used and which package source it came from; without the same tool version, the original plan cannot be continued.

Finally, infrastructure checks and service acceptance have to be kept apart. The verifier on page 27 asks five questions: are all nodes Ready, are they all on the target version, is the API service available, are any Pods stuck in an abnormal state, and are the admission webhooks still there? A Pod is the unit in which Kubernetes runs an application; a node showing Ready means it reports being ready to take on work. These five questions can support “the cluster meets these technical conditions,” but whether a given business transaction still succeeds needs the service's own verification. The slide's phrasing is “A Successful Command Does Not Mean a Healthy Cluster”; I would add one more line: cluster health is not service acceptance either. The platform team should hand the cluster evidence to the service owner, and the two sides decide together whether maintenance is over. Approval expiry also has to be split into four things: when to stop accepting new operations; whether operations already accepted run on to completion; when results may be confirmed; and who handles an overrun of the maintenance window. Expiry closes only the first; it must not erase effects that were accepted legitimately before expiry and confirmed complete only afterward, whether the work must finish inside the window is a separate contract to be stated explicitly, and the four are tested separately.

At this point approval, assessment and acceptance each have their evidence. But once the workspace is gone, which of that evidence can still be found? The next session answers from the storage layer.

**Whether an upgrade can end is decided jointly by an approval still valid for the current environment, completion evidence for every target, and the service's own acceptance.**

## 6. After the workspace restarts, which state should be restored?

Rui Su of Juicedata takes up an easily overlooked problem in “Stateful Sandboxes for Stateless Agents”: an agent's execution environment can be short-lived, while its working files and results may need to be kept for the next run ([official agenda](https://agntconmcpconjapan26.sched.com/event/2QlE7/stateful-sandboxes-for-stateless-agents-designing-durable-volumes-for-mcp-tool-workspaces-rui-su-juicedata), [public slides](https://hosted-files.sched.co/agntconmcpconjapan26/4e/Stateful%20Sandboxes%20for%20Stateless%20Agents%20%C2%B7%20designed-r3.pdf)). This session answers the third question of the handover: once the environment is gone, where does the successor learn how far the work got?

The sandbox this session uses is an isolated working environment that can be discarded with the task, like a workroom rented by the hour: the agent checks out code in it, edits, installs dependencies, runs tests, writes a report, and the whole room is cleared when it checks out. Isolation and restriction are what make it a sandbox; being disposable is this session's lifecycle choice. Page 2 uses those five tool calls to make the point that “The agent is stateless. The work is not.”: five calls accumulate a unique state on the same filesystem, and when the environment is gone after the tests, which things must stay and which should disappear? MCP specifies the format of a tool call, not the workspace behind the call: which directories are mounted, which are shared, which persist, and how to recover after a failure are still for the platform to decide.[10]

### First separate the data to keep from the permissions to recheck

The most intuitive approach is to save the whole directory. But page 4 warns that “Mechanism is not intent”: in the deck's words, “A volume can retain a secret too long and still lose an edit at the wrong commit boundary.” A directory holds original inputs, temporary files, finished artifacts, operation progress, and credentials all at once; sitting on neighboring paths does not mean they should share one lifecycle. Page 5 splits state into six classes, turning “keep it or not” into a set of choices that can be discussed.

📌【在此插入圖 asset-07.png】

Rui Su, Juicedata, slide page 5: inputs, working files, cache, artifacts, control state, and secrets each need their own way of being kept and recovered. Source: public conference slides.


Read-only, versioned inputs tell you where this round of work started; mutable working files let it resume after an interruption and let someone inspect the changes; cache can usually be regenerated even if it is lost; artifacts need explicit identification and a publishing step so that a half-finished product is not mistaken for a delivered one. The other two classes matter more. Control state covers who approved what and which operation started or ended, and the deck argues it belongs in an external, ordered, auditable record; secrets such as access credentials should be obtained at run time and never put into any snapshot. Putting control state outside and keeping secrets out of snapshots is a storage arrangement; it does not turn into a re-authorization flow by itself. Three more things have to go with it, and this is my suggestion: the approval record may be written only by a person or system with the authority; on taking over, query the authorization service for the conditions valid now; and if nothing can be found, stop at pending confirmation rather than carrying on with anything from an old snapshot. An external record can also be stale, or even rewritten by the agent, so “kept outside” is not a synonym for “trusted.” Only with those in place does restoring the working directory recover just the material the analysis needs, while whether the permissions are still valid is decided by the authorization service of the moment. This is also the design suggestion this section puts forward: credentials should not travel along with the working directory. The next session's deck shows one usable capability: different customers each have their own data scope (a tenant), and an agent can be issued a pass valid for one hour that allows reads and writes only under `/repo/docs`.[11] I would use it to design the re-credentialing step at remount, so that what the successor gets is short-lived, restricted access rather than the predecessor's credentials; the deck does not show the integration of re-credentialing with the transfer of the right to take over, and that part is my suggestion.

### Local progress and remote results can come apart at an interruption

Back in the upgrade flow, when the next agent reads a file saying “node A processed,” all it has is a statement left by the previous process. It still needs to compare that against the external operation record and then query the cluster's current state. If the request before the restart was sent but its result never got written back locally, resending may cause a duplicate action; if the local file recorded completion first while the remote side had not yet succeeded, skipping leaves work undone. Storage can keep the file, but the relationship between those two moments has to be handled by the operation workflow.

Reordering the file writes alone cannot close this gap, because the local write and the remote change each have their own moment of completion, and an interruption can still fall between them. Recovery needs to query the operations already sent and the target's state, then write what it finds back into the progress record. This is the application's recovery responsibility, and “the directory has a backup” cannot stand in for it.

### Define the durability promise first, then choose where to inject failures

Page 7 asks for durability to be written as a contract across the boundary. Think of saving a game: which action counts as a real save? How much progress is it acceptable to lose? How long until you are back at a point where you can keep playing? The deck's version is three promises, asked separately. First, when does it count as saved (the commit point): after which action, a write, a file close, or an `fsync` that forces data to disk, is an edit that has been acknowledged as “saved” truly safe from loss. Second, how much may be lost (RPO): how much acknowledged work may be lost; here I would split it again, since an analysis draft can be rebuilt while an operation identifier that has been sent must not be lost, and the two should not share one promise. Brought down to actual arrangements: plan drafts and analysis notes may stay local, operation identifiers and approval references must be durably registered before a request is sent, and finished reports have to be published to the artifact area; when the durability layer is unavailable, read-only analysis may continue while every operation that cannot be safely resent is stopped, and that is a design cost. Third, how fast is recovery (RTO): how quickly another sandbox can remount and read that edit. None of the three means the remote operation has completed. After that come retention periods and who is allowed to restore, fork, publish, or delete. Seeing an `fsync` call does not by itself let you assume every remote replica has completed.[10]

This also changes the tests I would choose. Page 13 says that “If you only benchmark throughput, you have not tested durability” and lists the failures to inject: kill the process to check the commit point and lost writes; kill the sandbox to check restart and remount; take down a node to see how long recovery from elsewhere takes; cut the network to check errors, retries, and concurrent writes; and confirm that secrets never enter a snapshot. I would add the cut points specific to the upgrade case: interrupt the process before the request is sent, after the remote side has completed but before the local record is written, and before the working files have been confirmed durable, then check whether recovery establishes the current state, whether it executes anything twice, and whether it loses data that was promised to be kept. This is a test design extended along the deck's direction; this article does not claim to have run it.

When work is handed to two agents at the same time, one more problem has to be handled: when both believe they hold the latest version, whose change gets to stay? That is the part the next session pursues further, starting from concurrent work.

**When recovering work, keep the material needed to continue, and recheck both the results of external operations and the current authorization before deciding the next step.**

## 7. No file was overwritten, and the work can still conflict

Tadatoshi Sekiguchi of PingCAP introduces two kinds of content that need to be kept across execution environments in “Externalizing Agent State”: the memory that lets an agent recall past work, and the files it actually produces. He uses the open-source mem9 and drive9 to show how memory, file storage, and multiple workspaces fit together ([official agenda](https://agntconmcpconjapan26.sched.com/event/2QlEe/externalizing-agent-state-memory-and-filesystem-management-for-sandboxed-coding-agents-tadatoshi-sekiguchi-pingcap), [public slides](https://hosted-files.sched.co/agntconmcpconjapan26/17/EN%20_GNTCon-Japan-2026_PingCAP%20Seki.pdf)). This session pushes the third question of the handover to the case where two people hand over at the same time, and it also answers the second: who holds the right to take over.

His starting point is the same as the previous session's: split the agent into the control program that reads the task, calls tools and moves to the next step (what he calls the stateless harness, which needs no local user data recovered after a rebuild) and the user's data that has to be kept separately, and put the data in a dedicated persistence layer, like the database behind a traditional application. drive9 provides a cloud workspace through a filesystem interface, so an agent can work with ordinary Unix tools and git; files are written to a local cache first and then uploaded to the remote asynchronously.[11]

### A version check keeps the edit, and keeps the chance to handle the conflict

Asynchronous upload means that what the agent can see locally is not necessarily what the remote has received; a recovery guarantee has to say which completion signal it waits for. The tenant and short-lived task pass were already borrowed in Section 6; here we look only at the files themselves.

The conflict handled on page 23 is much like two people editing the same shared document at once. A client reads a version number when it opens the file and carries that version when it writes back; if the version on the server has moved on, the write is refused, and the failed file stays on the side whose write failed. This is called optimistic locking: allow concurrent work first, and only at commit check whether this edit still rests on the same starting point.

📌【在此插入圖 asset-08.png】

Tadatoshi Sekiguchi, PingCAP, English deck page 23: a write carries the version read earlier, the overwrite is refused when the remote version has changed, and the failed local edit is kept. Source: public conference slides.


In terms of the upgrade plan: A and B both start from version 7; A fixes webhook compatibility and saves version 8, while B, still working from version 7, adds the worker steps. Accepting B's whole file would make A's change disappear; refusing B's stale write keeps the chance to recognize the conflict and, for the moment, keeps B's local edit; but a sandbox can disappear and uploads are asynchronous, so after the refusal the conflicting copy has to be saved first as a durably queryable artifact, and the environment cleaned up only once that save is confirmed, or B's edit can still be lost. Parallel exploration is worth that cost of merging and re-validating only when the branches really need to change the same thing at once; work that only reads needs no branch. But after the refusal, B still has to check the worker steps together with the new webhook conditions. The version number only knows that the starting point has changed; it cannot judge whether the two plans can hold together, and automatically rereading and then overwriting with the same content could wipe out the very change that was just protected. Conflict handling should keep both sides' reasons and differences before deciding to merge, redo, or hand the decision to a person.

Shared edits conflict easily; can exploration be isolated first? The deck answers with “layers” built with copy-on-write: two branches start from the same initial files, are modified on their own without affecting each other, and are finally committed back to the mainline or discarded, which suits an agent's exploration and trial and error. What it isolates is files: external operations, credentials and network effects are not isolated by it.

### Each file can be correct and the combination still violate the task requirement

Different files can also have no version conflict and still have a work conflict. Suppose the plan requires that only one worker be processed at a time. A and B each edit their own working file and each decide to start on a different worker. Every file is written from its latest version, yet together they violate the limit on concurrent operations. This example is my extension of the analysis: a file version protects an update to one piece of content, while the task requirement cares about the effect of two actions taken together.

Here the execution service can issue maintenance slots: a step with side effects first obtains a slot before it starts and releases it only once the operation is confirmed finished, while read-only assessment needs no slot and keeps running in parallel. That avoids locking the whole long-running analysis just to prevent operation conflicts. The scope of a slot has to be stated: one slot per node, one for the whole cluster, or slots issued against shared capacity? A single slot for the whole cluster is simplest and may slow things down needlessly; too narrow a scope fails to protect the task condition. The requirement in this case is that only one worker is processed at a time, so there is exactly one slot, issued and reclaimed by the execution service rather than each side reading a number of its own. If the coordinator itself fails, operations already accepted keep being tracked and new ones stop at pending confirmation. Serialization still leaves another question unanswered: A was only disconnected and comes back later, B has already taken over, and both hold a task approval that is still valid, so who may send the next step? The execution service must know who the current holder is, and someone must have the authority to designate them. The approach is to treat the takeover as a record updated by a trusted coordinator or an authorized person: the task, the current holder's execution identity (the identity of this run), the handover version and the scope of operations, all four updated together, with a consistent ordering between that update and the acceptance of requests. The handover version is not a number anyone who reads it can use; it is bound to the holder's identity: a request must carry both the version and the identity, and the execution service accepts it only if both match the current record. So when A comes back, even with an unexpired credential, it is refused because its identity is no longer the holder's; if A reads the latest version and sends with it, it still fails the identity check. Three races have to be tested: A's delayed old request arriving late, A impersonating the holder with the latest version, and B and C asking to take over at the same time. Checking a name or an account cannot handle these. Operations already accepted keep having their results tracked. Checking authorization and preventing two holders from acting at once are two different requirements; without the latter, a handoff has only passed data along and has not transferred responsibility for execution. This is this article's extended analysis. A request already sent whose result is unknown still needs an operation identifier and a result query; the fact that the file conflict has been resolved does not let you assume the external work has been picked up and completed as well.

### Memory helps recover the context; the approval still has to be looked up

mem9's memory is a freshness problem of a different kind: a memory summarized as “the engineer agreed to the upgrade” can only help locate the source of the approval at the time; it cannot replace an approval record that is still valid, and Part 3 opens this up.[11]

At this point the checks on authorization, the right to take over, state and completion are scattered across different components. Each component has its own security features, yet the business flow they form together can still drop a condition; the next session starts from exactly one observation of “ordered even though a condition was not met.”

**File versions protect edits and memory helps recover context, but whether an action may continue still has to be judged from the task scope and the current state.**

## 8. The components are all connected; who is responsible for the whole workflow?

Tatsuya Sato and Satoshi Ito of Hitachi use a procurement workflow in “What's Missing in the Open Agentic Stack” to compare ways of integrating different open-source components, discussing how agent communication, work dispatch, authentication, and observability connect, and who still maintains them once connected ([official agenda](https://agntconmcpconjapan26.sched.com/event/2QlEG/whats-missing-in-the-open-agentic-stack-lessons-from-oss-integration-tatsuya-sato-satoshi-ito-hitachi-ltd), [public slides](https://hosted-files.sched.co/agntconmcpconjapan26/bf/20260910_AGNTCon_Japan_Hitachi_Ito_Sato_public.pdf)). This session puts the four questions the earlier sessions took apart back onto a single work path.

The procurement case has clear steps: requirement, budget, compliance, approval, and ordering each have their own agent and system, and the expected rule is that “the order is placed only after every condition and the human approval are complete”; but on page 24 they record that in the demo an order was placed in some cases although a condition or approval had not yet been satisfied. In their terms, one procurement assignment is a task, each launch of an agent to work on it is a run, and a price lookup or an order is a tool call within it. The deck's technology survey is dated August 20, 2026, and should be read as a comparison of reference implementations at that time, not as a product ranking that stays valid.[12]

### Three ways to integrate: where does each put the maintenance responsibility?

The first approach wires A2A and MCP together directly: agent addresses are kept by the application as a static list, agents connect directly to each other and to tools, and Keycloak's token exchange passes the user's permissions downstream: like a purchasing role presenting its own ID to exchange it for a pass the target system accepts (Keycloak handles identity and access management); whether the pass's permissions have actually been narrowed has to be configured and verified separately, and holding the pass does not mean this order has been approved. This choice gives the team control over more of the details, and makes the team responsible for connecting them.

The second approach uses AGNTCY's shared facilities, which answer four questions in turn: whom to find, what it can do, how to pass the message, and what it has done (the component names are in the notes), so each team writes a little less integration code of its own. The deck cautions that proof of an agent's identity is not the user's delegation: knowing who is taking over, and knowing how much money it may spend on the user's behalf this time, are two questions that need different evidence.

The third approach uses agentgateway to centralize connection, authentication, and observability at a shared entry point. Page 20 says the gateway rewrites and distributes agent addresses, so callers need no code changes; traces, logs, and metrics for traffic that passes through the gateway can be collected without changing the agents; and it validates the origin and intended audience of access credentials at the entry point and controls access to agents and MCP servers on that basis; an entry point that validates the credential still does not know what this task was approved to do. The cost is written on page 23: there is no control outside the gateway, and how an agent advances the work internally cannot be seen.

In Section 3 of [The Harness Blueprint](https://medium.com/p/3facc281f633) I suggested that a first MCP gateway start from a tool inventory (a registry), authorization checks, a place to obtain restricted credentials (an identity broker), and an operation record (an audit log). Hitachi's third approach is close to it in where the central entry point sits, but end-to-end user delegation remains a gap, and page 20 lists it explicitly as something to build yourself. The missing piece of data is “what this task was approved to do,” and the component that should obtain and enforce it is the one that executes the operation, not the entry point that merely validates credentials. Treating the three approaches as material for selection, I would first look at which stretch of maintenance the team is prepared to take on: with few counterparts and fixed endpoints, direct integration keeps the necessary paths clear, but the connection and observability code has to be maintained in-house; when agents are frequently added or replaced, discovery and description mechanisms answer the problem directly; and only when several teams need to share access policies does a central entry point have a maintenance case. A shared entry point also concentrates the impact of outages and policy errors: when the entry point is unavailable, read-only queries can wait while change operations must stop, and the two deserve different handling. The selection table also has to answer three questions directly: which component reads the approval, which component refuses the order, and which team maintains the conditions; how convenient the connectivity is cannot answer any of the three. These are selection conditions I derive from the comparison; the architecture names in the deck cannot simply be ranked from better to worse.

### Shared components still need the business-condition checks wired in

What all three leave behind is responsibility for the business conditions. Page 24 lists three gaps: end-to-end observability is heavy, with the gateway recording “who accessed what” and OpenTelemetry (a standard for recording how programs execute) recording “why,” which is the slide's summary: to rebuild the timeline of one procurement from assignment through the agent's processing to the ordering service, every segment has to be instrumented in advance and the records correlated, and a timeline only proves the order of events, not an approval, so either on its own leaves an audit blank; there is no consistent approach to end-to-end delegation and authorization, so when the Agent → Agent → Tool → Resource path is decided dynamically, whose permissions to use, what to delegate downstream, and how to narrow at each hop still have to be built in-house; and the observation from the opening: an order placed although a condition or approval had not yet been satisfied.[12]

📌【在此插入圖 asset-09.png】

Tatsuya Sato and Satoshi Ito, Hitachi, slide page 24: the A2A task, agent run, and tool call need to be linked, permissions narrowed step by step, and the procurement flow verified to actually respect its conditions and approvals. Source: public conference slides.


The deck gives no count or denominator from which a failure rate could be calculated, and I will not inflate it into a claim that every related implementation will fail. But the example is enough to overturn the inference that “every component has security features, so the whole workflow must respect the approval.” A procurement approval may limit the amount, the items, and the deadline; an upgrade approval may limit the cluster and the steps. If the connecting component never obtains and checks that information, it has no way of knowing whether the well-formed request in front of it exceeds the task. Selection therefore has to include “who implements this check, and where does its data come from.” One more layer down: if the conditions for ordering are already known, why let a model decide whether an order may be placed at all? Put the conditions into the ordering service's deterministic state transitions, so that an incomplete set of conditions never reaches an orderable state, and let the agent handle collecting data and the exceptions; the price is that someone has to maintain those business rules.

### Link the task, run, and tool call back to the same authorization

One assignment may pass through retries and handoffs, so the links among the three levels, task, run and tool call, need to be kept. I would trace back from that order: which task triggered which run, which run issued which tool call, and which approval was it relying on at the time? These identifiers have to be related to one another before anyone can find the step at which the requirement was dropped. Complete records, though, only make the investigation afterward easier; to prevent a recurrence, the boundary that executes the order has to check the necessary conditions before sending and stop the action when they fail. A record that can be traced to a person is still not the same as someone taking the case. In Section 2 of the [organization piece](https://medium.com/p/92343384d987), the responsibility matrix (RACI) gives the shared environment and permissions to the platform, and the domain's MCP tools, product quality and responsibility for quality and incidents after release (production ownership) to the team that owns the product (the domain team); applied to a handoff: the platform maintains the shared mechanisms for operation identifiers and execution coordination, the domain team owns the semantics of each operation, its state queries and its remediation contract, and the service owner defines business acceptance and the risk that is acceptable; a result that remains unknown after a timeout is picked up by the on-call engineer, but the service owner makes the final call on recovering, stopping, or proposing a separate remedy.

What holds for procurement holds for the upgrade. The trust boundary of Section 3, the task limits of Section 4, and the approval binding of Section 5 join here into a single chain, and the handover version from Section 7 adds the last link: each tool call maps back not only to an approval but to the current handover version, so a late request from the predecessor finds nothing to hang on.

**Once the components are connected, the system still has to check the task and the approval before every external action, and leave a record that can be traced back to the person responsible.**

## 9. If I were doing the acceptance now, I would start from one interrupted handoff

Once Section 8 has joined up the chain of responsibility, one interrupted handoff can test the whole chain. The acceptance question I find most useful is this: **with the predecessor absent, or with the predecessor back again, can the next one still make a well-grounded decision?** I would start in a disposable test environment and accept only one kind of limited operation. What follows is a test design drawn from these decks, with no results from an actual run; nor is it an upgrade manual that can be used directly on a production cluster.

### Define the operation and the evidence contract first

Before starting, the platform owner writes down the designated cluster, the plan version, the permitted steps, and the approval conditions, and defines what can be queried in the target system once this step succeeds. The test apparatus independently records the operations it receives, their identifiers, and their actual effects, for the reviewer to check the agent's account against; the agent taking over may use only the query interfaces the execution service normally provides, and may not read the moment of failure the test apparatus privately recorded. First run one normal flow without interruption to confirm that the permitted work can actually finish; otherwise a system that rejects every change would also look very safe in the failure cases.

The design requires the execution service to accept a pre-generated operation identifier, so the caller can persist it before sending the request. An identifier on its own does not resolve the gap between a side effect and its record, so before adopting this the service's minimal contract has to be confirmed: one identifier maps to fixed parameters, and resending it with a different node or plan is refused; a query can distinguish five states, not accepted, running, completed, failed, and undeterminable; and how long records are kept, and whether the service still recognizes them after its own restart, are written down. These are preconditions for adoption, and they cannot hide behind the words “the service promises.”

Next, specify what this step is. This article tests only “upgrade node N” on one worker, which is three sub-steps: drain the workloads (`kubectl drain`), upgrade (`kubeadm upgrade node`, followed by updating and restarting the kubelet), and reopen the node to work (`kubectl uncordon`), and each has a different resend contract. The normal effect of a drain is that the workloads on the node are moved off; sent a second time, the node may already be empty, or it may evict again because the predecessor had already reopened it. The official documentation says only that `kubeadm upgrade` can be rerun; the resend semantics of updating and restarting the kubelet have to be confirmed separately; reopening the node makes it accept work again.[9] So acceptance does not count calls; it observes effects: whether any unapproved eviction or maintenance action occurred, whether the service disruption exceeded what was allowed, and whether the node ended up on 1.31, checked against the operation identifier and the node's state. If the test environment deliberately requires exactly one drain, that is a convention of this fixed environment, not a general rule of Kubernetes.

The record that travels with the handoff also has to be filled in first, or readers are left to translate the whole article into an interface themselves. For this test, all of it hypothetical data, with the writer of each field stated:

### After an interruption, how to decide the next step

Now let this operation complete in the target system but withhold the result from the agent, and interrupt its workspace. The new agent gets only the saved inputs, the plan, the working files, and the operation identifier; credentials are obtained afresh, the current approval is queried afresh, and the handover version is updated. This cut point is worth testing because the effect has already happened remotely while no successful response arrived locally; the most intuitive move, “rerun from the last line,” may add an extra eviction in this operation. The expected behavior is to query the operation already sent and the target's state first, confirm that the result meets this step's requirement, and then pick up the work not yet done. Acceptance compares against the external record: was one extra change issued and treated as new work, was the whole cluster wrongly marked complete, and does the handoff report say which data it checked?

The genuinely hard cut point is in the middle: the drain has completed, the upgrade has failed, the node still cannot accept work, and the maintenance window is about to close. The state the successor finds is “drain completed, upgrade failed, node still cordoned,” but “upgrade failed” does not mean the node is still cleanly on the old version, let alone fit to carry work. Remediation has to be chosen from three by the state actually found, and every one of them has to map back to the approval: if the node is confirmed unmodified and healthy, still on 1.30 with the kubelet normal and enough capacity, it may be reopened to work on the old version and marked unfinished; if the node has been partly modified, repair it by the approved procedure, for instance by retrying the upgrade inside the maintenance window; if the state is unclear or unhealthy, keep it isolated and hand it to a person. Once the approval has lapsed, none of the three may be sent by the agent on its own; only the case owner decides, and what they need to know is that this node cannot carry work right now, how large the impact is, and who has the authority to act outside the maintenance window. All of that belongs in the handoff record under “permitted next steps,” not in a bare “stop the dependent steps.”

One more cut point tests the execution service itself: interrupting only the agent never shows whether the service, having lost an operation record, produces the effect a second time. “Accepted” has to be taken apart first: request received, durably registered, acceptance replied, effect produced, result saved, and each of the five moments can be interrupted. The contract is that durable registration must precede the acceptance reply. So after a restart between receiving the request and registering it, the correct result is that the identifier is unknown and no effect occurred; after a restart following the acceptance reply, the query must find the record. If the service replied with acceptance but nothing can be found, it has broken its own promise, which is not normal recovery.

Now move the interruption to just before the request is actually sent. The reviewer knows that no operation happened this time, but the successor cannot use that answer directly. It has to query the saved identifier and interpret the reply according to the execution service's contract: “not found” may mean not yet executed, or it may only mean the record is not yet visible or has passed its retention period. Only when the query can establish that nothing was executed, or the service promises that resending with the same identifier will not add a second effect, is there reason to send, and then only after rechecking the current state and the authorization.

The local summaries of these two cases, “completed remotely but the reply was lost” and “the request was never sent,” can look very similar while the remote facts differ. A test that checks only “the task eventually succeeded after recovery” cannot tell whether the successor established the situation or just happened to rerun without anything going wrong. In the [Payment Walkthrough](https://medium.com/p/20452944263b) of Green Is Not Done, I used buying a bottle of green tea to make the point: when the payment screen freezes and the same request is sent again, it should get back the original result, not charge a second time; the property that resending the same operation with the same parameters within the retention period adds no effect is called idempotency. That article's code implements only sequential resends inside one process, and the open questions across restarts are already listed in its Section 9; what this article adds is a change of successor and a multi-stage infrastructure operation. If the tool offers neither a queryable identifier nor a defined safe way to resend, a handoff path for manual verification should be kept.

Once the successor has shown it can decide to continue or stop from this evidence, change the approval conditions: let the original plan be superseded by a new version, or let the approved maintenance window have ended. Keeping the old files should help the analysis recover, but it must not bring an expired approval back into force. Credential expiry is tested separately: obtaining a credential again may restore only the access that is still approved now; it cannot add authorization to the task. The right to take over is tested too: let the predecessor come back after B has taken over and send one step, and it should be refused because its handover version has expired; a late-arriving old request from the predecessor should fare the same. And check the verifier against a state where the control plane has met the target but the workers have not, to confirm that it honestly leaves the unfinished work on the list.

Monday's work can be assigned directly to three roles: a Staff engineer on the platform writes the contract for a single step, stating the moment of acceptance, the operation identifier, the successor's identity, and the query and resend semantics; QA and SRE arrange four cut points for the same step, before sending, after the effect but before the reply, a service restart, and the old holder returning, and keep the external-effect records; the service owner decides how much pending-confirmation time and manual handling can be tolerated, and narrows the scope of automation when they are exceeded. These cases are necessary verification items before expanding authorization, but they cannot support expansion on their own: the G2 gate in Section 5 of the Reliability article looks at the task set, repeated performance, the observation period and the review workforce, and the operations piece further separates “opening it to more teams” from “authorizing high-risk operations.” “Some recovery behavior conforms to the contract” and “the team is ready to take on more operations” are two different decisions.

## 10. Closing: the work left behind should be work the next person dares to take on

What the six decks changed is how I understand a handoff: giving the same data to the next agent does not mean the permissions, the external facts, the completion conditions and the right to take over have been handed over as well.

The cost most easily underestimated is making the incoming shift rebuild work that has already been done. The working files are there, but no one knows which plan was approved; the report says complete, but the node observations are nowhere to be found; the credential still works, but it is unclear whether the original approval covered only assessment or changes as well. These gaps turn the time automation saved into the next person's verification time. If they are discovered only inside the maintenance window, that person has to confirm the service's state with one hand while guessing what the predecessor did with the other.

A good handoff can therefore stop explicitly at not-yet-finished: it keeps the effects already produced, the results still to be confirmed, the original approval, the current handover version and the next place to query, so that whoever takes the case knows where to pick up and how soon it has to be handled. The next article asks one step further on: once the task is over and the experience left behind is passed to the next task, how much of it can still be trusted?

I will start by picking one small task that colleagues often have to take over from each other, put it through a deliberately arranged interruption, fill in, item by item, the evidence that recovery could not find, and write down who picks up a result that is still unknown after a timeout, how soon it must be handled, and who decides whether to recover or stop. Back to the Kong demo from Part 1, the one stopped by the rate limit: when a different agent takes over, beyond the conversation it also needs the record of the mock operations already completed, a scope of approval that is still valid now, and an entry point where results can be queried; the role-group permissions in the demo are not a task approval record, and that is a capability a recoverable system still has to add. What is worth continuing to accumulate after Tokyo is exactly this kind of work: work that others can inspect, correct, and hand on to the next person.
